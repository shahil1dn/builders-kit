# Behavioural-Science Canon (pre-verified)

Effects below were located from primary sources and put through an adversarial replication check (one independent verifier per effect, tasked to *kill* the claim). **Canon entries need no new web verification** — cite them directly. Each carries the disconfirming evidence found, the boundary conditions where it holds vs. fails, and documented side-effects. Read those before applying: an effect's tier tells you how much to trust it, the boundary conditions tell you whether it applies to *this* page.

Tiers: `REPLICATED` (survived independent replication / supportive meta-analysis) > `FIELD-VALIDATED` (strong large-N real-world data, thinner lab-replication record) > `CONTESTED` (mixed record — recommend only with the dispute stated). Debunked and folklore effects are in [blocklist.md](blocklist.md), never recommend them.

## Attention & Visual Hierarchy

### First-impression speed (50ms visual-appeal judgment)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Visual appeal of a web page is judged within ~50ms of exposure, and these snap judgments correlate ~0.9 with ratings given after 500ms — meaning the first split-second largely sets the user's aesthetic verdict; later replications pushed the threshold down to 17ms with visual complexity and prototypicality as the main drivers.

**Apply:** The above-the-fold first paint must look good instantly: clean layout, low visual clutter, conventional/prototypical structure (logo top-left, nav across top, hero with clear focal point). Optimise the hero's static first frame (no reliance on scroll or animation to 'redeem' a busy page), because the appeal verdict is cast before the user reads anything or interacts.

**Evidence:** Lindgaard, G., Fernandes, G., Dudek, C., Brown, J. (2006), *Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!*, Behaviour & Information Technology, 25(2), 115-126 — N: Three lab studies, university-student samples (~22-31 per study), tachistoscopic presentation of real homepage screenshots. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01449290500330448

**Study context:** University students (Carleton University, Ottawa, Canada); each study a separate small cohort; Lab, controlled tachistoscopic exposure (50ms and 500ms) of homepage screenshots; Not a purchase task — aesthetic appeal rating only; applies to landing-page/considered-purchase first contact

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No failed replication or null meta-analysis found after targeted adversarial search — the core finding survives. The legitimate weaknesses are scope and interpretation, not effect existence: (1) The famous "~0.9" is consensus/reliability across raters and repeated ratings averaged over participants, NOT proof a single user's verdict is permanently fixed at 50ms — individual variance is larger. (2) The original "mere exposure" explanation was itself challenged and needed follow-up experiments (Lindgaard's own ~2011 work using paradigms to rule out post-stimulus-offset processing) to defend. (3) The downstream leap — that snap visual appeal drives trust/credibility/usability/conversion via a halo — is a SEPARATE claim with a genuinely MIXED record: aesthetic→trust correlations are weaker and context-dependent (early halo work r≈0.28-0.39; some recent studies find aesthetic treatment raises attractiveness but NOT trustworthiness). Marketing usage routinely overreaches the 50ms finding into this contested territory.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: aesthetic-APPEAL judgments of full-page visuals (homepage/landing-page screenshots) form within 17-50ms and are stable across exposure durations; main drivers are low visual complexity and high prototypicality (looks like a typical site of its category). Measured on university-student lab cohorts (Carleton/Ottawa; later Swiss/Basel and large online samples) under controlled tachistoscopic single-screenshot exposure. The 2014 Reinecke worldwide-preference work shows complexity/colourfulness optima shift by CULTURE/age/education — so the *target* of "appealing" is not universal even if the *speed* is. DOES NOT cleanly transfer to: (1) trust/credibility/usability/conversion outcomes — that is a separate, weaker, contested halo claim, not the 50ms result; (2) live browsing with scroll, task intent, and interaction (ecological validity untested — it was static screenshots); (3) impulse-vs-considered purchase distinctions — no purchase task was run at all, it is pure appeal rating, so "sets the buying verdict" is an inference, not a finding; (4) text-heavy or content-driven pages where the value isn't visual-first.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Low dark-pattern risk: optimising for fast positive first impression (clean layout, low visual complexity, category-prototypical structure) is pro-user, not manipulative. The real failure mode is OVER-INDEXING on the snap verdict — chasing a pretty 50ms hero at the expense of substance, clarity, or content that a considered-purchase visitor actually needs. Prototypicality is a double edge: leaning into category convention raises instant appeal but caps distinctiveness, so for brand-led work it trades against differentiation. Do not cite the 50ms figure as evidence for trust or conversion lift — that's the unsupported extrapolation.

### Von Restorff / isolation (visual salience)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** An item made distinct from its uniform neighbours — by colour, size, shape, spacing, or isolation — is disproportionately noticed and remembered; the effect depends on the isolate differing against a backdrop of similar items, not just on being bright in absolute terms.

**Apply:** Make the primary CTA the single visually isolated element: one accent colour reserved for it, surrounded by neutral/uniform UI, with whitespace isolating it. The corollary is the constraint — isolation only works if everything else is visually uniform, so a page with five 'pop' colours has zero von Restorff effect. Use it for the one action you most want clicked; don't dilute it.

**Evidence:** Hunt, R.R. (1995), *The subtlety of distinctiveness: What von Restorff really did*, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2(1), 105-112 — N: Theoretical re-analysis of von Restorff (1933); effect itself replicated across decades of list-memory experiments (e.g. age-difference replications, Bireta/Surprenant/Neath 2008). https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03214414

**Study context:** Lab memory-experiment participants (students and, in replications, older adults); Lab list-learning/recall paradigms; distinctiveness manipulated via physical or semantic isolation; Memory/attention paradigm, not a purchase task — applied to web by analogy to focal-element noticing and recall

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No registered-replication failure or zero-effect meta-analysis found — the phenomenon survives. But three substantive published limits cut against the broad "noticed AND remembered" framing: (1) RECOGNITION FAILURE — "there is rarely an isolation effect for recognition, which is inconsistent with intuition and all known theories of memory" (Springer/M&C literature); the effect is reliable in FREE RECALL but frequently absent in recognition, and a 2025 study (PubMed 40788721) reexamines exactly this free-recall vs recognition vs source-memory split. (2) SERIAL-POSITION DEPENDENCE — early-list isolates often fail; Bireta/Geraci "Revisiting von Restorff's early isolation effect" (2017) found early-isolation effects absent or smaller than midlist for conceptual contrasts, because no prior context exists for the isolate to violate. (3) STRATEGY/AGE MODERATION — participants using mnemonic strategies show "little to no isolation effect" (individual-difference confound), and effect size is reliably smaller in older adults (Bireta, Surprenant & Neath 2008). No single theory (attention / distinctiveness / retrieval-cue) fully accounts for it across conditions — the mechanism is contested even though the phenomenon is not.

**Boundary conditions:** WHERE IT HOLDS: lab list-learning with FREE RECALL as the test; isolate placed at/after midlist so a uniform context is already established; physical/automatic contrasts (a number among words) transfer most robustly across positions; works across age but attenuated in older adults. WHERE IT WEAKENS OR DOES NOT TRANSFER: (a) recognition tests — effect often vanishes, so a 'will be remembered when re-encountered' claim is weak; (b) early-list / no-prior-context isolates; (c) users who already chunk or strategically encode (the salience adds nothing); (d) when MANY items are 'isolated' — distinctiveness is relative, so over-using accent colour/size destroys the contrast it relies on (the claim's own 'against a backdrop of similar items' caveat). APPLIED-TO-WEB CAVEAT: all primary evidence is memory-for-word-lists, not visual scanning of an interface or purchase behaviour. The 'disproportionately NOTICED' (attention-capture) half rests on visual-search salience literature, not on these recall studies; the 'remembered' half is recall-specific and recognition-fragile. Treats web focal-element hierarchy by analogy — directionally safe for drawing the eye to one CTA against uniform surroundings, but no field/purchase-task validation in the cited record, and no impulse-vs-considered, price-band, or culture data exists in this literature at all.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Salience is a finite resource and the effect is COMPARATIVE: each additional emphasized element (second accent colour, extra bold, competing badge) dilutes the contrast and can null the effect — over-isolation backfires. Drawing attention/recall to one element necessarily suppresses neighbours, so isolating a non-primary element (a decorative badge over the real CTA) actively misdirects. No dark-pattern risk inherent to the technique, but it can be abused to make a manipulative option (pre-ticked upsell) the visually dominant 'isolate'.

### Picture superiority effect  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Pictures are recognised and recalled substantially better than the equivalent words — Standing's classic study showed ~83% recognition of 10,000 photos seen once for 5s each, and the picture advantage holds across recall, recognition, and implicit memory tasks (dual-coding: images encode via both visual and verbal channels).

**Apply:** Carry key messages with concrete, meaningful images, not text alone: show the product in use, real photos over stock abstractions, icons paired with labels for scannable feature lists and process steps. Pair (don't replace) words with pictures so the dual-coding benefit compounds. Strongest for memorability/brand recall and for making value props stick after the user leaves the page.

**Evidence:** Standing, L. (1973), *Learning 10,000 pictures*, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 25(2), 207-222 — N: Lab recognition study; observers shown up to 10,000 photographs (theory grounded in Paivio's dual-coding, replicated widely). https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14640747308400340

**Study context:** Lab participants (small N per condition; the '10,000' is stimuli, not people); Lab recognition-memory test, single-trial picture vs word encoding; Memory paradigm, not purchase; applies to recall/brand-memory and scannability rather than direct conversion

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No failed replication of the core phenomenon found — the picture > word memory advantage replicates across free/serial/cued recall, item and associative recognition. The disconfirming evidence targets two SUB-claims, not the headline: (1) MECHANISM. The claim attributes the effect to dual-coding. Higdon, Neath, Surprenant & Ensor (2025, QJEP, doi:10.1177/17470218241235520) argue it is physical DISTINCTIVENESS, not dual coding: with colour pictures vs black words they got the standard advantage (d=0.895), but making words distinctive (varied fonts/colours/sizes) and pictures non-distinctive (B&W) ELIMINATED the effect (d=0.061, Bayes factor favouring null). They state 'no mechanism within dual-coding theory can explain the elimination of the picture-superiority effect.' So the parenthetical 'dual-coding' explanation in the claim is CONTESTED. (2) 'Holds across implicit memory tasks' is FALSE as stated. Weldon & Roediger (1987, Mem&Cog) and others show the effect REVERSES on data-driven/perceptual implicit tests: word-fragment completion priming ~26% for words vs ~11% for pictures (words win). It also reverses under short response deadlines (familiarity-driven) and is wiped out by deep semantic encoding (pleasantness rating equalises pictures and words) and by high inter-item visual similarity. The CMU lab even titled a paper 'Memory for pictures: sometimes a picture is not worth a single word.' On the famous '~83%' number: the headline figure is real-order-of-magnitude correct but loosely cited — Standing's largest 10,000-image condition retained a substantial-but-lower fraction (recognition declines as set size grows / over delay), so '~83% of 10,000' is a rounded popularisation, not the exact reported value for that specific condition.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: explicit recall and recognition of pictures vs their word equivalents, lab single-exposure encoding, concrete picturable items, standard (recollection-based) retrieval with adequate response time. For typical web projects this maps to brand/icon recall and scannability — a relevant, real-time-tested heuristic for making key info recognisable later. DOES NOT TRANSFER / REVERSES: (1) data-driven implicit tests (word-fragment/perceptual priming) — words win; (2) very short response deadlines / speeded familiarity judgements — can reverse; (3) deep semantic encoding for both modalities — advantage shrinks to ~zero (words already encoded richly); (4) high visual similarity among the pictures (e.g. a set of near-identical product shots) — advantage diminishes or reverses, because distinctiveness is the real driver; (5) abstract/non-picturable concepts — no usable picture, effect moot; (6) when the to-be-remembered content IS verbal (price, claim, CTA copy) — a picture can't substitute. Mechanistically: the effect tracks DISTINCTIVENESS, so a generic stock photo among many similar images buys little, while a distinctive, atypical image buys a lot. This is a memory/recognition effect, NOT a direct conversion or purchase-intent effect — no price-band or impulse-vs-considered evidence here; do not claim it lifts sales, only that it aids later recall/recognition.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Design implication, not a behavioural side-effect: leaning on imagery for information that is actually verbal/numeric (pricing, legal terms, the specific CTA) can REDUCE recall of that content, since words beat pictures on verbal/data-driven retrieval. Also, generic/low-distinctiveness imagery (typical stock photos, near-identical product grids) yields little memory benefit and adds page weight — the gain comes from distinctiveness, not from 'an image' per se. No dark-pattern risk; the effect is about memorability, not coercion.

### Banner blindness  `FIELD-VALIDATED`

**Claim:** Users systematically fixate away from anything that looks like an advertisement — right-rail boxes, top banners, bright/animated promo blocks — even when it contains genuinely useful content; eye-tracking shows almost zero fixations inside ad-shaped regions across task types.

**Apply:** Never style important content (key CTAs, value props, trust signals, promo offers) like an ad: avoid isolated right-column boxes, full-width top banners, gaudy colour-blocked rectangles, carousel/banner formats, and ad-network dimensions (728x90, 300x250). Put conversion-critical elements inside the main content column in native, non-ad-shaped layouts.

**Evidence:** Benway, J.P. (origin); Pernice, K. & Nielsen, J. (NN/g eye-tracking corpus) (1998 / 2007-2018), *Banner Blindness: Old and New Findings (NN/g eye-tracking research overview)*, Nielsen Norman Group field eye-tracking studies (hundreds of pages, task-based) — N: Hundreds of pages across multiple eye-tracking studies; task-driven sessions (qualitative heatmap evidence, participant counts not published per study). https://www.nngroup.com/articles/banner-blindness-original-eyetracking/

**Study context:** General web users performing real information-seeking tasks (e.g. retirement-investment research); Field/lab eye-tracking with task flows (not passive viewing); Mixed; spans informational and transactional tasks

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Strong form (almost zero fixations across task types) is contested. Hervet et al. 2011 (Applied Cognitive Psychology, Is banner blindness genuine) found the majority of participants fixated ads at least once per visit; what is suppressed is deliberate engagement and conscious recall, not gaze. Lee and Ahn 2012: ads still influence behaviour without awareness. Ortiz-Chaves et al. 2014: image presence did not change ad interaction. NN/g 2018 itself concedes large mobile inline ads catch some gazes. Defensible claim is avoidant fixation plus near-zero recall, not literal zero gaze. No meta-analysis with a single pooled effect size exists; corpus is many small-N eye-tracking studies.

**Boundary conditions:** Strongest for goal-directed information-seeking tasks (the cited population), right-rail and top-leaderboard positions, classic bright/animated ad-shaped blocks, experienced users with learned ad schemas. Weaker or absent for aimless browsing (avoidance drops; animated ads can attract free browsers), large mobile inline ads that fill the viewport (unavoidable gaze), content just below the nav bar, and text-style or content-congruent ads (lower blindness, better recall). Incidental fixation still occurs and influences behaviour even without recall. Genuine content shaped like an ad is glanced-at-then-dismissed, not never-seen. Native ad blindness emerging (Frontiers 2025) as users learn sponsored cues. Task and visual-schema driven, not documented as population/price/culture specific.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Avoiding ad-shapes for real navigation and CTAs is the legitimate takeaway. Risks: (1) disguising functional elements can shade into deceptive native-ad styling and erode trust; (2) since users do incidentally fixate but fail to recall, the belief they will never see it is false reassurance: ad-shaped content is seen, judged an ad, and dismissed (a comprehension/conversion loss, not invisibility). No dark-pattern risk in the effect itself; the risk is the remedy bleeding into disguising paid content.

### Processing fluency (ease-of-processing drives liking)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** The easier a stimulus is to perceive and process, the more positively it is judged — fluency from high figure-ground contrast, symmetry, prototypical/familiar layouts, visual priming, and readable typography reliably increases aesthetic pleasure and positive evaluation.

**Apply:** Make the page easy to read at a glance: high text/background contrast, legible body font at adequate size, symmetric/aligned grid, conventional layout patterns users already know, generous whitespace. Fluency also explains WHY the 50ms appeal verdict lands the way it does — a fluent page is an instantly-liked page. Reduce cognitive friction in copy (short sentences, familiar words) to lift perceived trust and quality.

**Evidence:** Reber, R., Schwarz, N., Winkielman, P. (2004), *Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure: Is Beauty in the Perceiver's Processing Experience?*, Personality and Social Psychology Review, 8(4), 364-382 — N: Theoretical review/synthesis integrating many prior experiments (not a single-sample study). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_3

**Study context:** Synthesis across multiple lab experiment populations (students/adults); Review of lab experiments (contrast, symmetry, font readability, priming manipulations); General aesthetic/evaluative judgment; applies to both impulse and considered contexts

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The strong "reliably increases liking, monotonically, across all manipulations" framing has multiple published cracks. (1) The cited 2004 Reber/Schwarz/Winkielman paper is a THEORETICAL REVIEW, not an experiment, so the question is whether the underlying findings replicate. The original perceptual-fluency-on-affect effect (Reber et al. 1998, Exp 1) was small — Cohen's d ≈ 0.24. (2) Several fluency offshoots failed rigorous replication: Goncalves/Reber-style "perceptual fluency lowers moral wrongness" failed across 5 studies / 694 participants (all null; Sci Direct, Cognition); the disfluency-improves-memory branch (Diemand-Yauman 2011 ugly fonts; Sans Forgetica) failed replication across 13 experiments / 1000+ participants and a study titled "Fortune is fickle." (3) Complexity/challenge DIRECTLY contradicts a monotonic fluency law: harder-to-process art gets liked MORE by experts and after deliberate processing ("When Challenging Art Gets Liked," PLOS One 2015 — challenging "mastery" portraits gained liking only via repeated evaluation, ruling out familiarity; Pleasure-Interest model, Frontiers 2024; Berlyne inverted-U). Reber later had to add a perceptual-vs-conceptual-fluency patch to save the theory from these cases. No clean failed registered replication of the core fluency→liking link itself was found, which keeps it out of DEBUNKED.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS BEST: snap/first-glance evaluative judgments by non-experts of relatively simple, low-stakes visual stimuli (logos, layouts, type readability, product/web aesthetics) — i.e. impulse and low-involvement contexts where the easy-on-the-eye default dominates. For most web work this is the legitimate zone: clean figure-ground contrast, readable body type, prototypical/conventional layouts, and symmetry will on average lift first-impression liking and trust. WHERE IT BREAKS / REVERSES: (1) Experts and connoisseurs — they can like, and often prefer, harder/complex stimuli (conceptual fluency from grasping meaning outweighs perceptual ease). (2) High-involvement / considered evaluation with deliberate processing — repeated, reflective engagement flips challenging stimuli to MORE liked. (3) Complexity & novelty domains (fine art, music, fashion, "interest"-driven categories) — inverted-U, not monotonic; too-fluent reads as boring/generic. (4) Attribution: when the viewer can see WHY it's easy (obvious simplicity), the fluency cue gets discounted. (5) Over-exposure: familiarity-driven liking decays past a peak. Caveats: measured mostly on Western university-student/adult lab samples; cross-cultural generality untested. Practical translation: use fluency to win the first 50ms (contrast, readability, convention) — do NOT over-extend it into "simpler is always better/more liked," especially for premium, expert, or interest-led brands where intentional friction/complexity can raise appeal.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Pushing fluency too hard produces generic, forgettable design — the "AI-slop" failure mode. High fluency lifts first-glance liking but can suppress interest, memorability, and perceived effort/skill (which Althuizen 2021, ~1800 ppts, found mattered MORE than cognitive ease for product-design appeal). Fluency also independently inflates perceived TRUTH and trust (illusory-truth / "fluency = truth" effect) — a manipulation-adjacent side effect to flag when applying it to claims/copy, not just visuals. Net: treat as a first-impression lever, not a master rule; pair with intentional distinctiveness so the design isn't easy AND empty.


## Choice Architecture & Decisions

### Default effect (opt-out vs opt-in)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Pre-selecting an option as the default roughly doubles the rate at which people end up 'choosing' it, because most people accept whatever is already set rather than actively switch; in Johnson & Goldstein's online experiment effective organ-donor consent was about 42% under an opt-in default vs about 82% under an opt-out default, and the opt-out condition did not differ from a no-default neutral condition.

**Apply:** Pre-tick the option you can ethically defend as best for most users (the recommended plan, monthly-not-annual only if genuinely better for them, newsletter OFF by default for trust). On forms, make the lower-friction safe path the default and require an explicit click to leave it. Do NOT pre-check consent/marketing boxes — that is both a dark pattern and unlawful under UK GDPR/PECR, which require active opt-in.

**Evidence:** Eric J. Johnson, Daniel G. Goldstein (2003), *Do Defaults Save Lives?*, Science, 302(5649), 1338-1339 — N: Online experiment N=161; plus cross-country natural-experiment data on national organ-donor registration. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1091721

**Study context:** 161 online respondents (US-based web sample) for the controlled experiment; national populations of 7 European countries for the field/natural-experiment comparison; Online randomised experiment (hypothetical organ-donor decision with manipulated default) plus archival cross-country field data on actual registration rates; Non-purchase consent decision (organ donation); generalised by the authors to insurance, privacy settings, and pension enrolment defaults

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No evidence kills the CHOICE-LEVEL effect — it survived adversarial search. But two serious caveats narrow the claim: (1) Publication-bias critiques of the wider nudge literature (Maier et al. 2022; Szaszi et al.) show that after correcting for severe publication bias, the broad "nudges work" effect shrinks toward zero — though the DEFAULT subcategory is consistently among the most robust nudges and survives better than most. (2) The claim's implicit real-world payoff ("save lives") does NOT replicate: a longitudinal natural experiment across 5 countries that switched opt-in→opt-out (Argentina, Chile, Sweden, Uruguay, Wales; data to 2019) found NO increase in actual deceased-donation rates, and a 2025 PNAS Nexus study found a "crowding-out" effect — opt-out gave a statistically insignificant +1.21/million deceased donors but a significant ~29% (-4.59/million) DROP in living donors. Steffel/Williams/Pogacar "Defaults are not a panacea" formalizes this: defaults strongly move the registration CHOICE but the effect attenuates or vanishes at the downstream OUTCOME.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: low-stakes / low-involvement decisions where the chooser has weak or unformed preferences and the action friction of switching is non-trivial — exactly the organ-donor consent, privacy-setting, insurance-add-on, and pension-auto-enrolment cases the authors generalized to. Effect is largest in consumer domains (Jachimowicz). For web work: opt-in vs pre-checked newsletter/marketing/cookie consent, default plan/tier selection, default add-ons — the registration-level effect is real and large here. DOES NOT TRANSFER WELL: (1) high-involvement, high-price, considered purchases where the user has strong prior preferences (the default gets overridden); (2) any case where the metric that matters is a downstream OUTCOME rather than the immediate click (donation registration ≠ donation; sign-up ≠ retention/activation) — the effect attenuates across that gap; (3) environmental-domain defaults are weaker. LEGAL/ETHICS BOUNDARY: pre-ticked consent for marketing/non-essential cookies is unlawful under UK GDPR/PECR (consent must be active opt-in) — so the strongest commercial application of this effect is exactly the one you can't use for consent. Pre-selection is fine for non-consent product choices (default plan), not for legal consent.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Two distinct downsides. (1) Reactance / trust erosion: in the organ-donation field literature, presumed-consent imposed by government is perceived as coercive and can trigger active opting-out and reduced public trust; the crowding-out finding (opt-out suppresses living donation) is a concrete backfire. For web UX, aggressive pre-selection of paid add-ons reads as a dark pattern and damages trust/brand. (2) Legal exposure: pre-ticked consent boxes are an outright UK GDPR/PECR violation, not just a soft side effect. (3) The choice-vs-outcome gap means a default can inflate a vanity metric (registrations, sign-ups) without moving the real goal (active users, completed donations) — measure the downstream outcome, not the click.

### Anchoring (price/quantity anchoring)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** An initial number shown to a person biases their subsequent numeric judgment toward that number even when it is arbitrary and even when they know it is arbitrary; in a supermarket field experiment, multiple-unit price framing and explicit purchase-quantity anchors raised units bought, e.g. a '12 for $X' or 'limit 12 per customer' frame increased average purchase quantity versus no anchor.

**Apply:** Show a higher reference price struck through next to the sale price; lead a pricing table with the highest (or a clearly-labelled enterprise) tier so cheaper tiers read as reasonable by comparison; use quantity-multiple framing ('Buy 4 for $20', 'most pick 3') and honest 'was/now' or RRP anchors. Anchor must be real/defensible — fabricated 'was' prices are unlawful price-comparison practice in the UK (CPRs / Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act).

**Evidence:** Amos Tversky, Daniel Kahneman (1974), *Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*, Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131 — N: Original demonstrations small-N lab; effect is one of the most replicated in psychology. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124

**Study context:** Original: university student lab samples (Tversky & Kahneman). Field validation: real supermarket shoppers (Wansink, Kent & Hoch 1998, 'An Anchoring and Adjustment Model of Purchase Quantity Decisions', J. Marketing Research 35(1), 71-81, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224379803500108); Lab estimation tasks (TK 1974) plus in-store field experiments on purchase quantity (Wansink et al. 1998); Spans low-cost impulse grocery items (field anchoring on quantity) to general numeric/willingness-to-pay judgments; strongest where the buyer lacks a firm internal reference price

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Two distinct problems, neither fatal to the mechanism but both fatal to the claim as framed. (1) The cited FIELD VALIDATION is Brian Wansink (Wansink, Kent & Hoch 1998, JMR). Wansink is among the most discredited researchers in the discipline: 18+ retractions, a 2018 Cornell research-misconduct finding (data misreporting, p-hacking, hypothesizing after results, failure to preserve data), and resignation. The 1998 purchase-quantity paper itself has NOT been formally retracted and is still cited, but it issues from a lab whose data-generating process was found fraudulent — so the headline 'limit 12' field numbers (3.3 cans with no limit -> 7.0 cans with limit 12, +112%) cannot be treated as clean field evidence. (2) Anchoring on PREFERENTIAL / ECONOMIC VALUATION judgments — i.e. willingness-to-pay and purchase quantity, exactly the domain this claim invokes — has a genuinely mixed replication record, in contrast to the rock-solid lab estimation effect. Fudenberg, Levine & Maniadis (2012) found NO significant WTP anchoring for any of six consumer products. Maniadis, Tufano & List (2014) failed to replicate Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec's (2003) 'coherent arbitrariness' anchoring of valuations. (There is a Simonsohn/Simmons/Nelson 2014 rebuttal arguing the 'failure' is statistically consistent with the original, so it is an unresolved live debate — not a clean win for either side.) So: arbitrary-number anchoring on valuations/purchase is materially weaker and less reliable than the famous cognitive-estimation effect the claim borrows its authority from.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: classic numeric estimation tasks (general-knowledge quantities, length/probability judgments) with a comparative-then-absolute format; self-generated or experimenter-given anchors on the SAME dimension as the target; judgments where the person lacks a firm internal reference value. Robust across populations, cultures, expertise, incentives, and forewarning. APPLIES MORE WEAKLY / CONTESTED: willingness-to-pay, willingness-to-accept, and purchase-quantity decisions — i.e. the exact e-commerce/retail application implied here. Direct WTP replications have failed (Fudenberg et al. 2012; Maniadis et al. 2014). LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: incidental/irrelevant random numbers planted away from the judgment dimension (numeric priming) — reduced-to-null in meta-analysis; buyers with a strong internal reference price (Wansink's own final study showed shoppers who retrieve an internal anchor neutralise the promo anchor — so frequent/branded/familiar-price purchases resist anchoring); high-deliberation considered purchases where the buyer has firm market knowledge. PRACTICAL READ for a retail/landing-page setting: a 'multi-unit price' or 'limit N per customer' frame is a plausible lever, but its real-world purchase-quantity payoff rests on Wansink field data that is now scientifically compromised; treat the lab mechanism as real and the specific purchase-quantity magnitudes as unverified.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Quantity-anchor framing ('limit 12 per customer', 'buy 10 for $X') is not a dark pattern per se, but it nudges buyers toward over-purchasing relative to need — risk of post-purchase regret, returns, and waste, especially on perishables. It is most effective precisely where the buyer is least informed (no internal reference price), which raises a fairness flag for low-knowledge or vulnerable segments. Multi-unit pricing must not obscure or inflate the genuine per-unit price (UK: misleading-price / CPRs concerns). Use as a mild quantity suggestion on genuinely discounted items; avoid manufactured scarcity or fake 'limits' on items with no supply constraint.

### Choice overload (boundary-conditional, NOT universal)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Adding options does NOT reliably hurt conversion on its own — the Scheibehenne et al. (2010) meta-analysis found a mean effect near zero across 50 experiments. But the larger Chernev, Bockenholt & Goodman (2015) meta-analysis (99 observations, N=7202) shows a real, significant overload effect once four moderators are present: high choice-set complexity, high decision-task difficulty, high preference uncertainty, and an effort-minimising decision goal. Overload is a conditional effect, not a law.

**Apply:** Don't blindly cut options. Reduce the felt complexity instead: add filtering/sort/search, group items into categories, surface a 'recommended/most popular' pick, and provide comparison aids and good defaults — these neutralise the moderators that actually cause overload. Trim raw option count specifically where the user has weak preferences and just wants to get through (e.g. a checkout add-on or a plan picker), not in a catalogue a motivated shopper is browsing.

**Evidence:** Alexander Chernev, Ulf Bockenholt, Joseph Goodman (2015), *Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis*, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333-358 — N: Meta-analysis: 99 observations, N=7202. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1057740814000916

**Study context:** Aggregate of consumer-choice experiments (students and general consumers) across product categories: jams, chocolates, mutual funds, consumer electronics; Meta-analysis of lab and field choice experiments; includes the Iyengar & Lepper (2000) Draeger's supermarket jam field study (6 vs 24 jams; 30% vs 3% purchase) as one demonstration, not as standalone proof; Both impulse (jam/chocolate tasting) and considered (mutual funds, electronics); overload strongest for complex/uncertain considered choices made with an effort-minimising goal

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three serious disconfirming threads. (1) Decline effect: the famous Iyengar & Lepper (2000) jam field study (6 vs 24 jams, 30% vs 3% purchase) has a poor replication record — direct replications using jam in a German supermarket and using chocolates/jelly beans found NO effect; Scheibehenne et al. (2010) meta-analysis (~50 experiments, ~63 conditions, N≈5036) found a mean effect size of essentially zero. (2) The two foundational meta-analyses openly CONTRADICT each other and the dispute is unresolved — McShane & Böckenholt (2017, "Multilevel Multivariate Meta-analysis with Application to Choice Overload") was published specifically because Scheibehenne (~0) and Chernev (conditional effect) reached opposite conclusions due to different data simplifications. (3) p-curve reanalysis of the choice-overload literature reached "conclusions opposite that of existing tools," raising evidential-value/publication-bias concerns. Additionally, Chernev's four moderators emerged from meta-regression on heterogeneous studies (post-hoc moderator identification), and Chernev's own primary studies were partly designed to elicit overload — so the moderator structure is not an independent confirmation.

**Boundary conditions:** Holds (effect most reliable) where ALL of Chernev's enabling conditions co-occur: large/complex assortments with hard-to-compare, non-dominated alternatives; difficult decision task (time pressure, cognitive load); high preference uncertainty (buyer doesn't know what they want, no prior expertise, no articulated ideal point); and an effort-minimising/low-commitment goal (browsing, not committed to buy). Strongest in considered purchases under uncertainty (mutual funds, electronics, jam-tasting with no preference) and confirmed at LARGE assortment sizes in field e-commerce (the inverted-U: small increases in options HELP conversion; overload only bites past an inflection point). Likely does NOT transfer where: the buyer has a clear pre-formed preference or expertise (dominant alternative exists → more options just raise hit-rate); options are well-categorised/filterable/sorted (good choice architecture neutralises complexity); the assortment is small-to-moderate (adding options there typically lifts conversion); high purchase commitment; or culturally — most evidence is WEIRD (US/European students and consumers), transfer to other cultures is untested. Practical caution: do NOT cull product options as a default conversion tactic — the near-zero main effect means trimming choice is as likely to hurt as help unless the four conditions are actually present. Reach for it only on complex, uncertain, low-commitment decisions, and pair option-reduction with sorting/filtering/recommendation rather than raw removal.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Cutting options to "reduce overload" is a real downside risk, not a free win. Because the broad main effect is ~zero, removing legitimate choices on a page where the four moderators are absent can suppress conversion (fewer chances to match a buyer's preference) and frustrate high-intent/expert users who came for a specific item. Removing options can also read as a thinner/less-credible assortment. Safer lever than deletion: improve choice architecture (default/recommended pick, categorisation, filtering, comparison tooling) — the MSOM field data shows most of the overload damage comes from people never starting to search, which good defaults and curation fix without amputating the catalogue.

### Decoy / asymmetric-dominance (attraction) effect  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Introducing a third option that is clearly worse than (dominated by) one existing option but not the other shifts share toward the dominating option; Huber, Payne & Puto (1982) showed adding an asymmetrically dominated alternative violates choice 'regularity' and raises the target's choice probability.

**Apply:** In a 3-tier pricing layout, position a middle/target tier so an adjacent tier is obviously dominated by it (same or near price, fewer features) — this nudges share to the target without removing the user's freedom to pick anything. Classic execution: a print+digital bundle priced at/just under the print-only option makes the bundle the obvious pick.

**Evidence:** Joel Huber, John W. Payne, Christopher Puto (1982), *Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis*, Journal of Consumer Research, 9(1), 90-98 — N: Original lab studies, small-N student samples. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/9/1/90/1839380

**Study context:** University student lab samples in the original study; later tests across beer, cars, restaurants, gambles; Lab choice experiments using simplified options described on two numeric attributes (e.g. price x quality); Considered multi-attribute choices presented as clean numeric trade-offs; this attribute format is itself the effect's key boundary condition

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three independent lines of disconfirmation. (1) Trendl, Stewart & Mullett, "A Zero Attraction Effect in Naturalistic Choice" (Decision 8(1):55-68, 2021; SSRN 2018) is the first experiment built to satisfy Huber/Payne/Puto's OWN 2014 replication criteria for real-world choice and found a *precisely zero* effect. (2) Yang & Lynn (2014, JMR) ran 91 attempts across 23 product classes / 73 decoyed sets and got only 11 reliable effects — far fewer than chance-corrected expectation — and their meta-analysis explicitly frames the literature effect as fragile and inflated by methodological artifacts. (3) Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014, "The Limits of Attraction," JMR) argue the effect essentially only appears when every attribute is a printed NUMBER, and vanishes once any attribute is perceptual/experiential (taste a drink, see a shaded probability wheel). Much of the supportive literature is unincentivized/hypothetical. So: not zero everywhere, but the famous "robust regularity violation" framing does NOT survive contact with naturalistic, incentivized choice.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: lab/online choices where ALL attributes are presented as clean numbers (price x quality rating), considered multi-attribute trade-offs, typically hypothetical/unincentivized, decoy clearly and asymmetrically dominated, target not already dominant in share, traditional WEIRD student/online samples. The numeric-attribute format is itself the load-bearing boundary, not incidental. LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: experiential or perceptual attributes (taste, look, feel), naturalistic stimuli (movies by title, real snacks, photographed products), real money on the line, choices where the target already wins most share, and at least one nontraditional population reversed the meta-analytic pattern. A "repulsion effect" (decoy HELPS the dominated competitor) has been documented in some conditions. For real e-commerce / pricing-tier UI, treat as a plausible nudge in pure spec-sheet comparisons (numeric tiers side by side) but expect it to weaken or disappear for image-led, taste-led, or high-stakes purchases.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Used as a pricing-page tactic (a deliberately bad middle tier to push the premium tier) it is a known, somewhat transparent manipulation; sophisticated or repeat buyers can notice the "obviously worse" decoy and it can erode trust. Risk of the repulsion effect: a badly calibrated decoy can shift share AWAY from your target toward the cheaper competitor. Effect is unreliable enough in real purchase contexts that designing revenue projections around it is overconfident — treat as a low-cost A/B candidate, not a guaranteed lever.

### Hick's law (choice reaction time) — narrow, often misapplied  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** The time to choose among N equally-probable alternatives that are each mapped to a distinct response increases logarithmically (not linearly) with N: RT = a + b*log2(N+1). This is a robust law for speeded response-selection, but it is about response time in mapped-response tasks, not about decision difficulty or conversion in visually-scanned menus.

**Apply:** Use as a weak heuristic to keep primary navigation and toolbars lean and grouped (chunking flattens the log curve), so frequent actions are fast. Do NOT use it to justify hiding catalogue/search content or claim that 'fewer links = higher conversion' — for a scanned list users use visual search, not Hick's response-selection, so the log-RT model does not apply. Pair with grouping/labels rather than raw count-cutting.

**Evidence:** William E. Hick (1952); Ray Hyman (1953); reviewed by Robert W. Proctor & Darryl W. Schneider (1952), *On the Rate of Gain of Information (Hick 1952); review: Hick's law for choice reaction time: A review (Proctor & Schneider 2018)*, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 4(1), 11-26 (1952); review in QJEP 71(6), 1281-1299 (2018) — N: Original: single-digit-N lab subjects, many trials each; widely replicated for mapped-response RT. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28434379/

**Study context:** Small-N laboratory subjects performing speeded keypress tasks; not consumer/web populations; Lab reaction-time experiments mapping each stimulus to a distinct, practised response; Not a purchase effect at all — a perceptual-motor reaction-time law; web relevance is by analogy to navigation/menu choice only

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The log-linear form is not a clean, universal law. (1) Range-restriction artifact: the canonical RT = a + b·log2(N+1) fit holds because nearly all studies stay within 2-8 choices; extend beyond ~3 bits and the function becomes curvilinear, and a power curve fits as well or better (Longstreth, El-Zahhar & Alcorn 1985, who concluded "in our opinion, the law is false"; Welford/Kvålseth "Hick's law: its limit is 3 bits"). (2) The slope is not invariant — it can be flattened to ~zero by high stimulus-response compatibility (Leonard 1959 found no RT difference across 2/4/8 choices with vibrotactile finger mapping) and is reduced by practice and stimulus intensity (Proctor & Schneider 2018 review). So b is a property of the mapping, not a constant of the mind. (3) Cross-species generality fails: a touchscreen Hick task did not reproduce the effect in pigeons (Vance et al. 2023, Behavioural Processes). (4) UX misapplication is explicitly documented: when an interface supports visual scanning or ordered/alphabetical lists, users search non-linearly and the predicted log-increase does not hold — the law is "most robust" only for random search with no scanning strategy (IxDF; NN/g "Designing Long Menu Lists"). The claim's own hedge ("not about decision difficulty or conversion in visually-scanned menus") is correct and is itself the main published criticism of the popular UX usage.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: small-N lab subjects making speeded keypresses where each stimulus maps to one distinct, practised response, set size roughly 2-8 (≤3 bits), equiprobable alternatives, low/moderate S-R compatibility, and where users cannot pre-plan or scan. There the RT-vs-log(N) increase reproduces. BREAKS / does NOT transfer: (a) high S-R compatibility — slope collapses toward zero; (b) N beyond ~8-16 — function turns curvilinear, log form is wrong; (c) unequal option probabilities; (d) any visually-scanned interface (web nav menus, alphabetical/ordered lists, dropdowns) where users scan rather than choose-from-uncertainty — this is the dominant misapplication; (e) considered vs impulse decisions, conversion, or "decision difficulty" generally — the law says nothing about these; it is a perceptual-motor reaction-time law, not a model of preference, deliberation, or purchase. No price-band, purchase-type, or culture dimension was ever measured — it was never a consumer/commerce effect. Web relevance is by analogy only; treat any conversion claim built on it as unsupported.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Designing UX around "fewer choices = faster/better" via Hick's law can backfire: collapsing options into nested menus or progressive disclosure adds navigation depth and clicks, and the log relationship means added options cost progressively less time — so aggressive option-cutting trades a tiny per-item time saving for worse findability. The law also does not predict choice satisfaction or conversion (those are paradox-of-choice / cognitive-load territory, separate effects), so using it to justify removing genuinely wanted options risks hurting task completion rather than helping it.


## Trust & Social Proof

### Review-count lift (volume of reviews drives conversion)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Displaying customer reviews on a product page raises purchase likelihood by roughly 270% versus a page with zero reviews, with most of the lift captured by the first five reviews and the effect markedly stronger for higher-priced items (~380% lift) than lower-priced ones (~190%).

**Apply:** Surface a visible review count and the reviews themselves on every product/pricing page; prioritise getting at least the first 5 reviews onto a product, and lean hardest on reviews for high-consideration/high-price items where the conversion lift is largest.

**Evidence:** Spiegel Research Center (Medill, Northwestern University) with PowerReviews (2017), *How Online Reviews Influence Sales*, Spiegel Research Center field study / industry report (analysis of transaction data from 3 online retailers) — N: Transaction-level data across three online retailers (two lower-priced categories, one higher-priced gifts retailer); exact unit count not disclosed in public report. https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/

**Study context:** Real shoppers on three live US e-commerce retailers (lower-priced consumer categories + one higher-priced gift retailer); field (observational analysis of live e-commerce transaction/conversion data); Both impulse and considered; effect is price-moderated and largest for higher-priced/considered purchases

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The famous 270%/190%/380% figures come from a single 2017 Spiegel Research Center + PowerReviews OBSERVATIONAL study (no causal identification; correlational analysis of live transaction data). Three independent lines of evidence cut against treating the magnitude as causal: (1) Endogeneity/selection bias — products that sell more accumulate more reviews, so the review-conversion correlation is contaminated by reverse causality. (2) The cleanest causal test, a large randomized field experiment on Airbnb (Fradkin & Holtz, "More Reviews May Not Help," 2021/2023 Marketing Science), found that experimentally induced first reviews produced only a temporary transaction bump that washed out — NO detectable effect on total nights sold or revenue — and that incentivized reviews were more negative, confirming naive observational estimates substantially overstate review impact. (3) The volume effect is the contested half of the academic literature: Floyd et al. (2014) meta-analysis found volume elasticity (Es=.41) far weaker than valence (Es=.78), and Babic Rosario et al. (2016, JMR) meta-analysis of 1,532 effect sizes across 96 studies found eWOM-sales correlation averaging only ~.091 (modest), reversing Floyd on volume-vs-valence — i.e. even academic effect sizes are small and the volume/valence ranking flips with operationalization. PowerReviews' OWN 2021 re-analysis (4.5B visits, 1,200+ sites) reported a 52.2% lift at 1+ reviews and a roughly LINEAR review-count-to-conversion relationship — contradicting the original "first five reviews capture most of the lift / rapid diminishing returns" framing.

**Boundary conditions:** Holds (directionally): the zero-to-some-reviews jump is a robust, real conversion driver across e-commerce, especially for considered/high-involvement and higher-priced purchases where uncertainty is high (Floyd: elasticities greater for high-involvement products; Spiegel: 380% vs 190% price split is directionally plausible). Third-party review sites show stronger sales elasticities than first-party (Floyd). Likely does NOT transfer as a precise 270% causal lift to: any specific page (the number is a cross-sectional correlation, not an A/B-test causal estimate); low-involvement impulse/commodity buys where reviews matter less; platforms where review presence correlates with listing age/popularity (selection bias dominates, per Airbnb); and the "front-loaded, plateaus after five reviews" shape is category-dependent and contradicted by PowerReviews' own linear curve (Home & Garden plateaus ~30 reviews; Furniture/Grocery keep climbing past 100). Culture: meta-analytic moderation by culture is documented (Qiu & Zhang 2023) but not characterized here. Practical takeaway: use as "ship some reviews, prioritize getting from 0 to a handful, weight higher-priced items" — do NOT quote 270%/380% as a guaranteed causal uplift in a client deck.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Soliciting volume aggressively backfires: incentivized reviews skew negative and do not improve buyer-listing matching (Airbnb RCT). Chasing 5.0 stars is counterproductive — purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0-4.7 and declines toward 5.0 (perfect scores read as fake), per the Spiegel page itself. Fake/over-curated review volume risks trust collapse and, in the UK, falls under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 ban on fake/incentivized-without-disclosure reviews (CMA-enforceable) — a real legal exposure for client e-commerce sites, not just a UX risk.

### Rating credibility sweet spot (4.0–4.7 beats a perfect 5.0)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Purchase likelihood peaks for average star ratings in the 4.0–4.7 range and declines as ratings approach 5.0, because near-perfect scores read as implausible/fake and a few negative reviews increase perceived authenticity.

**Apply:** Do not hide or filter out moderate/negative reviews to manufacture a perfect score; show the true average (which typically lands in the trust-optimal 4.2–4.7 band) and let a minority of critical reviews stand to signal authenticity.

**Evidence:** Spiegel Research Center (Medill, Northwestern University) with PowerReviews (2017), *How Online Reviews Influence Sales (Star Ratings and Review Content)*, Spiegel Research Center field study / industry report — N: Same multi-retailer transaction dataset as the review-count finding; exact n not disclosed. https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/

**Study context:** Real shoppers on three live US e-commerce retailers; field (analysis of live conversion data by displayed average rating); Mixed; spans lower- and higher-priced categories

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The single biggest problem: the SPECIFIC band in the claim is contradicted by the same vendor's later, far larger dataset. The original 2017 Spiegel/PowerReviews study put the peak at 4.0–4.7 (popularised as "4.2–4.5"). But PowerReviews' 2022 benchmark across 20M+ product pages on 1,000+ sites (data pulled 7 Mar 2022) found the conversion peak is at 4.75–4.99, NOT 4.0–4.7 — i.e. the optimum sits much closer to perfect than the claim states. Targeting 4.5 over 4.8 would leave conversion on the table. So the directional/non-monotonic finding holds, but the cited numeric sweet spot is stale and materially wrong. Provenance weakness: both data points come from ONE vendor (PowerReviews) + Northwestern partnership — not independent replication. The original study was an industry report / press release ("From Reviews to Revenue"), not a peer-reviewed publication; no sample size, model spec, or controls are public. The headline confound is unaddressed in public materials: average rating is correlated with review count, product age, price and category, so a raw "5.0 converts like 3.0–3.49" comparison may reflect that perfect-5.0 products are overwhelmingly low-review-count/long-tail items (5.0 is easy with 2 reviews, near-impossible with 2,000), not a credibility penalty per se. The "46%/53% distrust perfect ratings" stat is survey self-report (stated attitude), not behaviour, and is often spliced onto the conversion curve as if it were the mechanism. No registered replication or peer-reviewed meta-analysis tests this exact inverted-U curve.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it likely holds: US e-commerce product pages where an average star rating is displayed ALONGSIDE a visible review count, across mixed FMCG/general-merchandise categories — i.e. the exact PowerReviews context. The effect needs (a) a numeric average shown to 1–2 decimals and (b) enough reviews that a 5.0 reads as implausible. Where it likely does NOT transfer: low-review-count items (a 5.0 from 3 reviews carries no "too good to be true" signal — and may be where the raw 5.0 underperformance actually comes from, a confound not a credibility effect); services/local businesses and single-provider bookings where a 5.0 is normal and expected; non-US/high-collectivism cultures (review meta-analyses flag individualism as a moderator, so the skepticism-of-perfection mechanism may not generalise); considered/high-price purchases where buyers read review TEXT rather than reacting to the headline number; and any UI showing only stars without a count. Practical caveat for design use: do NOT engineer toward 4.2–4.5 on the strength of this claim — the better data says the real optimum is ~4.8–4.9; the actionable takeaway is "don't fear a handful of negative reviews / don't suppress them," not "aim below 4.7."

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Mechanism is credibility/authenticity inference, so it can backfire if used to justify manufacturing or tolerating negative reviews to "look authentic" — that risks crossing into deceptive practice and erodes the genuine signal. The honest application is only: surface real negative reviews rather than hiding them, and don't panic-optimise toward a sub-4.7 target. Also note the 5.0 underperformance may partly be a low-sample artefact, so "deliberately keeping ratings under 5" is unsupported advice.

### Descriptive social-norm messaging ("most people do X")  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Telling people what the majority of similar others actually do (a descriptive norm) produces more compliance than a generic appeal to the abstract benefit: a 'the majority of guests reuse their towels' sign lifted reuse to 44.1% vs 35.1% for a standard environmental message, and a same-setting 'provincial' norm ('guests who stayed in THIS room') pushed it to 49.3%.

**Apply:** Replace abstract benefit copy ('Help save the planet' / 'Join us') with a concrete descriptive-norm line citing real majority behaviour ('83% of customers chose the annual plan', 'Most people in [your city] pick X'); make the reference group as specific/similar to the visitor as the data honestly allows.

**Evidence:** Noah J. Goldstein, Robert B. Cialdini, Vladas Griskevicius (2008), *A Room with a Viewpoint: Using Social Norms to Motivate Environmental Conservation in Hotels*, Journal of Consumer Research, Vol. 35(3), 472–482 (DOI 10.1086/586910) — N: Two field experiments; Study 1 ~190 rooms / 1,058 potential towel reuses, Study 2 expanded reference-group conditions. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/35/3/472/1856257

**Study context:** Hotel guests at a US mid-priced national-chain hotel (real guests, real behaviour); field experiment (randomised sign conditions across rooms over ~80 days); Not a purchase — a low-cost compliance behaviour (towel reuse); applied by analogy to plan selection, sign-ups, donations

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Two strong disconfirming lines. (1) Direct replication failed: Bohner & Schluter (2014, PLOS One, two German hotels, N=724 + N=204) found descriptive-norm signs were NOT more effective than the standard environmental message, and the headline "provincial/same-room norm beats general norm" effect reversed in Study 1 (general 85.6% vs provincial 78.0%, p=.017) and vanished in Study 2. A 2023 study (ScienceDirect S026151771830205X line) also failed to replicate the provincial-norm advantage over a standard appeal. (2) Publication-bias correction nulls the effect: a 2025 Nature Human Behaviour meta-analysis of social-norms messaging for health behaviours (89 studies, 79 descriptive) found unadjusted Cohen's d=0.14 (95% CI 0.09-0.19) but after robust-Bayesian publication-bias correction d=0.01 (95% CrI 0.00-0.09), BF01 strongly favouring the null and BFpb=259.5 = "very strong evidence" of publication bias; authors recommend "alternative approaches." No descriptive-vs-injunctive advantage survived correction.

**Boundary conditions:** Holds (small effect) where: descriptive norm is truthful and the target's current behaviour is BELOW the stated norm (room to move up), repeated personalised feedback (energy reports) rather than one-shot signage, and a population not already saturated on the behaviour. Likely does NOT transfer: (1) high-baseline populations - Bohner attributes the German non-replication to already-high environmental concern (towel reuse already 82-93%), i.e. ceiling/no headroom; (2) people already performing ABOVE the norm, where descriptive norms trigger a boomerang (Schultz 2007 - low-users increased use), so a bare descriptive norm can backfire and needs an injunctive component; (3) one-off persuasion signage in general, where meta-analytic effect is ~0 after bias correction. The towel study is a low-cost compliance behaviour in a hospitality setting - extrapolating the precise 9-14pt lifts to plan selection, paid sign-ups, donations, or considered/high-price purchases is unsupported; treat any expected effect as small (low-single-digit percentage points at best) and condition-dependent, and never state a norm that is false.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Boomerang risk is the headline side effect: a descriptive norm tells people below AND above the norm where the middle is, so high performers regress toward it (documented in Schultz et al. 2007 energy study). Mitigation is pairing with an injunctive cue (approval signal). Stating a descriptive norm that is untrue, or that implies the undesirable behaviour is common ("most people don't pay on time"), normalises the bad behaviour and backfires - only use when the majority genuinely does the desired thing. Effect is small even when it works, so do not trade it off against clarity, honesty, or trust.

### Visual security/trust seals at the point of payment  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Adding a visible trust/security badge near sensitive form fields raises perceived security and reduces checkout abandonment driven by trust concerns (~18–25% of users abandon over credit-card security worries); strikingly, even a researcher-fabricated 'fake' seal increased perceived trust more than several real SSL seals, showing the cue works on visual presence, not verifiable authenticity.

**Apply:** Place a recognisable security/trust badge (Norton tested strongest) directly adjacent to the credit-card and other sensitive input fields — not buried in the footer — and visually reinforce the payment section, since users judge security per-region of a page rather than per-site.

**Evidence:** Baymard Institute (Christian Holst et al.) (2023), *How Users Perceive Security During the Checkout Flow*, Baymard Institute Checkout Usability research (qualitative usability testing + large-N abandonment surveys) — N: Checkout usability test sessions plus abandonment surveys of ~1,000+ US adult internet users; seal-comparison ranking across multiple survey waves 2013–2023. https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form

**Study context:** US adult online shoppers (representative-panel surveys + moderated usability test participants); field/online experiment + moderated usability testing on live and prototype checkouts; Considered e-commerce checkout (card entry); price-band agnostic

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The claim's headline mechanism — "the cue works on visual presence, not verifiable authenticity" — is contradicted by the peer-reviewed behavioral literature, which repeatedly finds the active ingredient is seal FAMILIARITY/recognition, not mere visual presence. (1) Kirlappos & Sasse, "Why Trust Seals Don't Work: A Study of User Perceptions and Behavior" (Springer/TRUST 2012): eye-tracking found ~38% of participants never noticed any seal, and those who did widely misunderstood its meaning ("a seal automatically legitimizes any site"). Conclusion: seals fail to fulfil their potential because they are neither noticed nor understood. (2) "Not all seals are equal" (Electronic Commerce Research 2015, online lab experiment n=300): the two seals were "not effective on average"; the null was driven by the LOW-dissemination (unfamiliar) seal, while only the HIGH-dissemination (familiar) seal raised purchase probability — directly the opposite of "authenticity doesn't matter, presence does." (3) Kimery & McCord (JITTA): TPA seals "not noticed or adequately understood." (4) Multiple practitioner A/B threads report unknown/obscure badges INCREASING anxiety/suspicion ("why haven't I seen this before?") and depressing conversion — a backfire mode the claim ignores. The fake-seal finding is real but narrower than stated: Baymard measured SELF-REPORTED perceived trust in a survey (Google Consumer Survey / SurveyMonkey Audience), not checkout behavior, and the homemade seal still imitated the visual GRAMMAR of recognized SSL/Norton seals — so it is not evidence that arbitrary visual presence works. Practitioner effect sizes are wildly inconsistent (Baymard ~11.5% avg uplift; some tests +42%; others a slight uplift for a COMPETITOR badge or no difference), with publication/case-study selection bias inflating the positives.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it HOLDS: (a) Perceived-security uplift from visually emphasising the card-entry region (padlock iconography, "secure" copy, a badge that LOOKS like the established visual seal grammar) — robust at the self-report/perception level. (b) FAMILIAR/recognized seals (Norton, PayPal, well-known SSL marks) raising purchase conversion in real US e-commerce checkouts — field-supported. (c) Considered card-entry purchases by US adult shoppers on lesser-known/SMB merchants, where baseline trust is low and there is room to move. Where it likely does NOT transfer: (1) The "fake/arbitrary seal works because authenticity is irrelevant" framing — overstated; UNFAMILIAR or invented badges are null at best and can BACKFIRE (raise suspicion / cognitive load), especially when they don't mimic recognized seal conventions. (2) Large household-name brands — most don't use seals; little headroom, possible negative signal. (3) Behavioral/conversion outcomes generalized from SURVEY perceived-trust data — the fake-seal result was perception, not measured purchase behavior. (4) Non-US / cross-cultural and EU markets — seal dissemination differs (e.g., Trusted Shops/Trustmark recognition is region-specific), so which seal is "familiar" changes the effect entirely. (5) Impulse/low-ticket purchases where trust friction is already low.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Backfire risk is real and under-acknowledged in the claim: unfamiliar or "official-sounding but unknown" badges (e.g., a made-up certifier) can INCREASE buyer anxiety and depress conversion by adding processing load and prompting "why haven't I seen this?" suspicion — the opposite of intended. Ethical/legal flag: deploying a literally fabricated security seal (vs. a real one earned) is a deceptive/misleading-impression risk under consumer-protection norms (UK CPRs / FTC). The defensible practice is using a RECOGNIZED, accurate seal and visually emphasising the genuinely-secure card region — not faking authenticity. Over-badging ("safety placebo" badge clutter, per Baymard) also dilutes signal.

### Source credibility via professional visual design (design = trust)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Users judge a site's credibility primarily from surface visual design: in a study of over 2,500 participants, 'design look' (layout, typography, colour, professional appearance) was the single most cited credibility factor, named in 46.1% of comments — far more than information accuracy or company reputation.

**Apply:** Treat polished, intentional visual design (clean layout, consistent typography, quality imagery, no broken/amateur elements) as a primary trust lever, not decoration; a generic or sloppy 'AI-slop' look directly suppresses perceived credibility before any copy is read.

**Evidence:** B.J. Fogg, Cathy Soohoo, David R. Danielson, Leslie Marable, Julianne Stanford, Ellen R. Tauber (2003), *How Do Users Evaluate the Credibility of Web Sites? A Study with Over 2,500 Participants*, Proceedings of the 2003 Conference on Designing for User Experiences (DUX '03), ACM (DOI 10.1145/997078.997097) — N: 2,684 participants evaluating 100 live websites across 10 content categories. https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/997078.997097

**Study context:** 2,684 general internet users (58% female, mean age ~40, 47 US states + 29 countries); online study — participants rated credibility of two randomly assigned live sites and wrote open-ended comments later coded into categories; Not purchase-specific — general credibility judgement across e-commerce, finance, health, news, etc.

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The strongest disconfirming evidence comes from the original author. Fogg's own Prominence-Interpretation Theory (Fogg 2003, credibility.stanford.edu) explicitly reframes the 46.1% figure: it measures PROMINENCE (what users notice and can articulate in open-ended comments) NOT what actually DETERMINES their credibility verdict. Fogg flagged two self-report biases in the same body of work: (1) rationalization — "what people reported did not actually influence their feelings but were the most likely explanations," and (2) memory limits — unremembered factors may have driven judgments "to an even greater extent." So "named in 46.1% of comments" does not establish design as the causal driver; it establishes it as the most salient/articulable cue. NN/g, summarizing this, notes the comment-coding study and the parallel survey studies (where users said contact info/privacy policies mattered most) flatly disagree — reconciled only by treating the 46.1% as prominence, not weight. Second, the claim's word "primarily" is contradicted within Fogg's own data: domain experts (health, finance) were "far less concerned about the surface aspects... and more concerned about the breadth, depth, and quality of a site's information." Third, Sillence et al.'s longitudinal field studies of real health-information seekers found a STAGED model: poor visual design predicts early REJECTION (mistrust), but it is content credibility and personalization — not design — that predicts SELECTION (actual trust). Design is a fast first-pass filter, not the determinant of the final trust decision. No registered replication or meta-analysis exists either confirming or killing the specific 46.1%/"primarily" claim — the number itself has never been independently replicated as a number.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it HOLDS: first-impression / rapid-rejection stage; lay (non-expert) users; visually-driven verticals — Fogg's own breakdown shows design comments highest for finance (54.6%), search engines (52.6%), travel (50.5%), e-commerce (46.2%); novel/unfamiliar sites with no prior brand trust; the fast, System-1 "do I bounce or stay" decision. Where it WEAKENS or does NOT transfer: (1) Expertise — domain experts (health, finance professionals) weight content depth/accuracy over surface design; (2) High-stakes considered decisions and the SELECTION (vs rejection) stage — Sillence shows content credibility, not design, drives the decision to actually trust/act; (3) Content-heavy verticals — design comments dropped for health (41.8%), news (39.6%), nonprofit (39.4%), and for health sites information focus rose to 33%; (4) Known brands — once reputation is established, design's marginal credibility role shrinks; (5) "Slick" backfire — overly polished design can read as marketing gloss and REDUCE trust for information-seeking users. Population/culture caveat: sample was 58% female, mean age ~40, predominantly US (47 states) plus 29 countries but English-language and self-selected online volunteers (2002 web aesthetics) — neither price-band nor purchase-type specific, and the visual baseline is two decades stale (modern users' design expectations have shifted).

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Treating "make it look professional" as sufficient for trust is the dangerous misread of this claim. Design gets you past the rejection filter but does not manufacture justified trust — and the same halo effect that rewards good design also lets scam/phishing sites borrow credibility purely through polish (the effect is cue-based, not truth-based). Over-polishing can trip the "slick = selling, not informing" backlash. For client work: use design to clear the first-impression bar and avoid early bounce, but pair it with genuine content/authority signals (clear sourcing, contact info, real expertise) for the trust-and-convert stage — do not claim design alone "primarily" drives credibility. The weak form is safe to act on; the strong form ("design over accuracy") is the part to not over-rely on.


## Pricing & Value Perception

### Free-shipping threshold (conditional free shipping)  `FIELD-VALIDATED`

**Claim:** Offering free shipping above a minimum order value is a highly effective lever on both order incidence and basket size; consumers are strongly loss-averse to a separately stated shipping fee, and a $0-shipping threshold pulls baskets up to clear it.

**Apply:** Set a free-shipping threshold modestly above average order value and surface a live progress nudge ('You're $8 away from free shipping') in the cart; bundle shipping into the product price where margins allow, since a salient shipping line-item is a top abandonment trigger. The threshold simultaneously exploits the zero-price effect (free shipping) and loss-aversion to the fee, while lifting AOV.

**Evidence:** Michael Lewis, Vishal Singh, Scott Fay (2006), *An Empirical Study of the Impact of Nonlinear Shipping and Handling Fees on Purchase Incidence and Expenditure Decisions*, Marketing Science, 25(1), 51-64 — N: Transaction database from an online retailer that experimented with a wide range of shipping-fee schedules (full customer-order panel; ordered-probability model). https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormksc/v25y2006i1p51-64.html

**Study context:** Customers of a real online retailer (direct-to-consumer e-commerce); field — econometric analysis of an online retailer's transaction database across naturally varied shipping-fee schedules; complemented by Baymard Institute field survey data on checkout abandonment; online retail orders across categories; effect operates on basket composition and reorder behaviour

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Two strands of disconfirming evidence. (1) The cited study's OWN conclusion undercuts the rosy framing: Lewis, Singh & Fay (2006) found free shipping and free-shipping-above-threshold "very effective in generating additional sales" BUT explicitly that the lost shipping revenues plus non-responsive segments render the promotions "unprofitable to the retailer." The demand lever is real; the net-value lever was negative in the original data. (2) The underlying mechanism — partitioned pricing / loss aversion to a separately stated shipping fee — is far weaker and more conditional once meta-analysed. Abraham & Hamilton (2018, J. Marketing Research) found an overall partitioned-pricing effect of only Cohen's d = .08 (p = .10, NOT significant at conventional levels), reversing negative when the total price is shown (d = -.23, all-inclusive wins), for hedonic products, and for atypical/unreasonable surcharges. A cinema-surcharge field experiment (Dertwinkel-Kalt, Köster & Sutter) found demand "independent of the price presentation," bounding the salience effect. So the strong, universal "consumers are strongly loss-averse to a shipping fee" mechanism is CONTESTED, even though the basket-clearing demand effect itself is well supported.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: online retail (direct-to-consumer e-commerce) where shipping is a genuine separately-charged add-on and the basket is incrementally extendable with low-priced filler; utilitarian/considered categories; price bands where the threshold sits ~15-40% above current AOV (one more item clears it); markets/cultures where free shipping is the expected norm (US/UK e-commerce) so a charged fee reads as a loss. Basket-pull and incidence effects are strongest here. WEAKENS / DOES NOT TRANSFER: (1) when the all-in total is shown salient and early (Abraham & Hamilton: effect goes negative, d=-.23) — modern transparent checkouts work against the loss-aversion story; (2) hedonic/luxury/single-item considered purchases that can't be padded to clear a threshold; (3) surcharges that are large or seem unreasonable relative to base price (backfire); (4) NET PROFITABILITY — the original study's key caveat: the demand lift can be wiped out by waived shipping revenue plus subsidising customers who would have bought anyway (cannibalisation). A threshold can lift AOV and incidence yet lose money if contribution margin on the incremental basket doesn't cover the absorbed shipping. "Highly effective lever on value" is true for top-line incidence/AOV, unproven-to-false for net margin without per-retailer margin maths.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Documented downsides beyond the original framing: (a) Margin erosion / negative ROI — absorbed shipping plus subsidising would-have-bought-anyway customers (cannibalisation) can make the promotion lose money despite higher AOV; set the threshold from contribution margin against the real basket distribution, not a round number. (b) Threshold gaming — customers add a cheap filler item to clear the bar then return it, inflating returns/processing cost. (c) Expectation ratchet — habituating customers to "free shipping" makes any future charged fee read as a penalty, so loss aversion later works against you. (d) Backfire when the total is shown early (Abraham & Hamilton) — on transparent modern checkouts the separated-fee tactic can depress preference vs all-inclusive pricing. (e) Hedonic/luxury mismatch — separating a shipping fee on a premium single item can cheapen perception and reduce intent.

### Charm pricing (9-ending / just-below pricing)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Setting a retail price to end in 9 (e.g. $39 rather than $34 or $44) raised demand by roughly a third in controlled field experiments, despite the higher absolute price, because the left-most digit dominates magnitude perception.

**Apply:** On product cards, pricing tables and plan tiers, price just below a round/leftmost-digit boundary ($39, $49, $199 — not $40, $50, $200) so the leading digit drops. Strongest on new items the visitor can't price-anchor independently; do NOT pair a 9-ending with a 'Sale'/'was $X' cue, which the same study showed cancels the lift.

**Evidence:** Eric T. Anderson, Duncan I. Simester (2003), *Effects of $9 Price Endings on Retail Sales: Evidence from Field Experiments*, Quantitative Marketing and Economics, 1(1), 93-110 — N: Three randomized field experiments across two national women's-apparel mail-order catalogs (tens of thousands of catalog recipients per cell; exact direct-mail cell sizes per item). https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1023581927405

**Study context:** US catalog shoppers (women's apparel), real purchase decisions; field — live mail-order catalog price manipulation, actual sales as the dependent measure; considered consumer-goods purchases, mid-price apparel (~$30-$80 dresses/clothing)

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three landing punches against the "roughly a third lift" framing. (1) Troll et al. (2024) meta-analysis, Journal of Consumer Psychology — preregistered, k=69 studies, m=362 effect sizes, N=40,541. Just-below prices DO raise purchase decisions, but the effect is SMALL: g=0.13, 95% CI [0.01, 0.25] — barely excludes zero, nowhere near a ~33% demand lift as a population effect. Effects are "highly heterogeneous," p-curve shows a large proportion of nonsignificant effects, and publication-bias corrections push the true effects "smaller and, at times, nonsignificant." (2) Macé/Sokolova-style preregistered online experiment, PLOS One 2022 (PMC9387778), 266 participants, 4,788 INCENTIVIZED real-money decisions, powered to detect a 5% effect — found just-below 47.1% vs rounded 45.9% vs control 45.3%, all non-significant (p>0.05); failed to reproduce both the left-digit AND perceptual-fluency effects. (3) Snir & Levy, "If You Think 9-Ending Prices Are Low, Think Again," Journal of the Association for Consumer Research 6(1), 98M+ scanner observations: 9-ending prices are on average HIGHER than non-9 endings (by up to 18%), undermining the clean "left-digit makes it look cheaper → demand rises" mechanism; the bargain image appears to be a learned association maintained by promotional use, not an automatic perceptual artifact.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it held: US catalog/mail-order shoppers, real purchases, mid-price considered apparel (~$30-$80), and — per Anderson & Simester's own moderation — strongest for NEW items where buyers lack an internal reference price, and WEAKER for repeat/previously-sold items. The effect was also weaker or absent when a "Sale" cue was present (the $9 ending and the Sale sign are substitute low-price signals; together they don't stack). Where it likely does NOT transfer: items with a strong existing price anchor or frequent repurchase (regulars mentally round); contexts already saturated with sale/discount cues; premium/luxury positioning, where round/whole prices signal quality and a .99 can cheapen perception (meta-analysis: zero effect on quality image, g=0.00, so 9-endings buy a price-image gain but not on prestige goods); sophisticated B2B or high-deliberation purchases. Cross-culturally, prevalence and meaning of 9-endings vary (e.g. USA vs Poland literature; in some markets 8 or other endings dominate), so the US-grocery/catalog magnitude should not be assumed abroad. Net: use a 9-ending as a cheap, low-risk default for novel mass-market consumer goods without competing discount cues — but do NOT promise a ~33% sales lift; the defensible expectation is a small, sometimes-null nudge.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Possible downsides to flag: on premium/quality-positioned products a .99 ending can undercut perceived quality/prestige (round-number pricing is itself used as a luxury signal); stacking a 9-ending with a "Sale" cue is redundant and can dilute both signals (Anderson & Simester); and Snir & Levy show 9-endings can mask genuine price increases — an ethically grey area if used to obscure a rise rather than signal a deal. No dark-pattern concern for the basic tactic, but the magnitude should be communicated honestly: small expected effect, not a guaranteed third.

### Zero-price effect (the power of free)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Dropping a product's price to exactly zero produces a discontinuous, disproportionate jump in demand far larger than the same-size price cut would predict at any positive price; in a real-purchase field test the cheap item's chosen share rose from 27% to 69% when its price fell from 1 cent to free.

**Apply:** Frame an offer around a genuinely $0 element — 'free shipping', 'free trial', 'free gift with order', 'free plan tier' — and make the word 'free' the salient anchor, rather than a near-equivalent token discount (e.g. prefer 'free shipping' over 'shipping just $0.99'; '$0/forever free tier' over '$1/mo'). Use a free entry tier to pull users into the funnel before a paid upsell.

**Evidence:** Kristina Shampanier, Nina Mazar, Dan Ariely (2007), *Zero as a Special Price: The True Value of Free Products*, Marketing Science, 26(6), 742-757 — N: Multiple experiments; Exp.2 real-purchase field study at an MIT student-center booth, ~243+ choosers across price conditions (per-cell t-tests t≈56 / t≈-32). https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.1060.0254

**Study context:** MIT students / passers-by (Exp.1 survey n=60; Exp.2 real chocolate purchases at a campus booth); lab + field — Exp.1 hypothetical survey, Exp.2 actual transactions with real money and real chocolates (Hershey's Kiss vs Lindt truffle); trivial impulse purchase (single chocolate, 1-15 cents); effect is demonstrated for low-stakes items, not high-consideration goods

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No registered/Many-Labs direct replication of the original chocolate paradigm exists; it was not in Many Labs 2. The headline claim that zero ALWAYS produces a disproportionate UPWARD jump is contradicted by a published reversal: Fan, Cai & Bodenhausen (2022, J. Acad. Marketing Science, 5 studies, N up to 3,502) document a "boomerang effect" — when incidental/non-monetary costs are HIGH (commute, wait, effort, risk), a zero price LOWERS demand vs a low price, the opposite of the prediction. Effect sizes there were small (ηp² ≈ .01–.06). Additional published null/context-failures: no zero-price effect in B2B contexts; little/no effect on online review ratings; the effect vanishes for purely utilitarian offers and for "critical" non-monetary costs like surrendering personal data; in a high-price single-product context the affect boost does not translate into a behavioural effect. So the effect is real but conditional, not the universal discontinuity the bare claim implies.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: trivial, low-stakes, hedonic impulse purchases where incidental/non-monetary costs (effort, wait, risk, data) are near zero — exactly the original setting (single chocolate, cents, instant pickup). Strongest for hedonic/pleasure goods; weaker-to-absent for utilitarian goods. LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER / REVERSES: high-consideration or high-price goods; offers with meaningful incidental costs (long commute, queue, signup friction, risk) where free can REDUCE demand (boomerang); B2B buying; anything requiring personal-data surrender as the 'price'; high-price single-product contexts. Population evidence is heavily WEIRD (MIT students / US lab + a few East-Asian neural and Chinese price-framing studies); little cross-cultural field validation. Practical caution: 'free' as a trust signal can also backfire — a field observation that identical homes advertised via a free service sold slower and cheaper than via a paid service, i.e. free can signal low quality.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Two documented downsides for a practitioner: (1) Boomerang — pairing 'free' with any non-trivial friction (forced signup, wait, data handover, shipping hassle) can depress conversion below what a small positive price would get. (2) Quality-signal backfire — 'free' can connote low value/quality in considered or reputational contexts, lowering perceived worth, willingness-to-pay later, and conversion. Both mean 'set price to £0' is not a free win outside the low-friction hedonic-impulse band.

### Incidental price anchoring  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** An arbitrary, logically irrelevant price encountered nearby (an unrelated product) shifts willingness-to-pay for the target item in the direction of the anchor; boardwalk shoppers paid more for a CD when an adjacent sweatshirt was priced $80 rather than $10.

**Apply:** Place a high-priced 'anchor' item adjacent to the item you want to sell — show the premium/enterprise plan or a deluxe SKU first/leftmost so cheaper options read as reasonable; lead a pricing page with the most expensive tier, or surface a high 'compare at' figure near the real price. Works by seeding a high reference number the visitor's mind hasn't already priced.

**Evidence:** Joseph C. Nunes, Peter Boatwright (2004), *Incidental Prices and Their Effect on Willingness to Pay*, Journal of Marketing Research, 41(4), 457-466 — N: Field study n=60 boardwalk shoppers wanting a specific CD; sweatshirt anchor alternated $10 / $80 every 30 min (plus supporting lab studies). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkr.41.4.457.47014

**Study context:** US beach-boardwalk consumers intending to buy a specific music CD (field); plus lab samples; field — real point-of-sale on a boardwalk, vendor alternated an adjacent unrelated product's price; reinforced by controlled lab experiments; low-to-mid-price impulse/considered purchase (music CD); anchor was an unrelated apparel item

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The specific mechanism behind this claim — an anchor from a DIFFERENT, irrelevant dimension (a sweatshirt price) shifting WTP for an unrelated target (a CD) — is exactly the variant that fails to replicate, even though classic same-dimension anchoring is robust. (1) Klein et al. (2014) Many Labs direct replication of incidental anchoring: d = .04, essentially zero. (2) Doğerlioğlu-Demir et al. (2020), Collabra: Psychology — three high-powered pre-registered studies across multiple product categories (tech, holidays, entertainment) FAILED to detect any incidental anchoring effect on price estimation; Bayesian analyses favored the null. (3) Replicability-Index z-curve audit of the incidental-anchoring literature: published success rate 73% but expected discovery rate only 6% (at chance), verdict 'either very small or zero'; the Nunes & Boatwright boardwalk study itself yields only BF10 ≈ 1.45 ('anecdotal', barely above 1). (4) Schley & Weingarten '50 Years of Anchoring' meta-analysis (2,603 effect sizes) finds classic anchoring robust at d=0.824 but explicitly flags 'reduced (or null) effects associated with incidental anchoring, anchors from different dimensions or from random numbers.' The one partial defence: Kvarven et al. (2020) bias-corrected meta-analysis retains d≈.40, and a car-auction field study shows d≈.2 — so it is not a clean zero, hence CONTESTED rather than DEBUNKED.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it might hold: classic, deliberate, SAME-DIMENSION anchoring (you show a high reference price for the SAME or a similar product, e.g. a struck-through 'was £80' on the item itself, or a high-priced premium tier next to the target) is well-replicated (d≈0.82) and safe to use — but that is NOT what this claim describes. Where it likely does NOT transfer: (a) anchors from an unrelated/different product dimension (the sweatshirt-to-CD leap) — this is the failed-replication zone; (b) when the buyer has product knowledge or a strong internal reference price (moderator that kills the effect); (c) under incentives, deliberation, or debiasing (considered purchases, B2B, high-ticket items where buyers research) — incentives reduce it; (d) numeric priming from random/incidental numbers generally washes out. Most fragile for considered/high-value purchases and knowledgeable buyers; any residual effect is most plausible only for low-stakes impulse buys with no internal price reference, and even there the field evidence is near-anecdotal. Culture/population: tested mainly on US/Western consumer samples; no robust cross-cultural validation. Do not bank conversion strategy on placing an arbitrary unrelated high price near a target item — the honest expectation is a near-zero effect.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** No meaningful upside means little to trade off — but note that fabricating an irrelevant high 'anchor' price near a product (e.g. an unrelated expensive item staged purely to inflate WTP) edges toward a misleading-pricing dark pattern with negligible evidenced payoff. Legitimate same-dimension reference pricing (genuine RRP/was-price) is the replicated, defensible alternative, and in the UK is regulated under CMA/DMCCA pricing rules (the 'was' price must be a real prior price). Use real reference anchors, not incidental ones.

### Partitioned pricing (base + surcharge vs all-inclusive)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Splitting a total into a base price plus a separately stated mandatory surcharge (vs one combined price) lowers consumers' recalled total cost and can raise demand, because shoppers under-process or anchor on the base component; recalled cost was $78.27 partitioned vs $83.90 combined.

**Apply:** When you must charge add-ons (handling, service fee, setup), itemising them with a low headline base price can lift recalled affordability and conversion — and presenting the surcharge as a percentage rather than a dollar figure further reduces the share who mentally add it up. BUT see side-effects: for web checkout this collides directly with the surprise-cost abandonment problem, so partition with the total still visible up-front.

**Evidence:** Vicki G. Morwitz, Eric A. Greenleaf, Eric J. Johnson (1998), *Divide and Prosper: Consumers' Reactions to Partitioned Prices*, Journal of Marketing Research, 35(4), 453-463 — N: Exp.1 auction n=199 grad business students (15% buyer's premium vs none); Exp.2 phone-choice n=233 undergrads (combined vs $-surcharge vs %-surcharge). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224379803500404

**Study context:** Graduate and undergraduate US business students (lab); auction with real bidding and a hypothetical phone-purchase choice; lab experiments (penny-jar auction with buyer's premium; brand-choice task) — not a live e-commerce field test; considered purchase (consumer electronics / auction lot); surcharge framed as buyer's premium and as shipping/handling-type fee

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The strongest disconfirming evidence is the Abraham & Hamilton (2018, Journal of Marketing Research, 55(5), 686-703) meta-analysis — 27 papers, 149 observations, N=12,878. It is the dominant authority and outranks the 1998 original. Its findings gut the unconditional claim: (1) The pooled MAIN effect of partitioned vs all-inclusive on consumer preference is tiny and NOT significant: Cohen's d ~= 0.08, p = .10. So "lowers recalled total cost and raises demand" is not a reliable general effect — it is roughly zero on average. (2) The effect SIGN-FLIPS under the conditions that dominate real e-commerce. When the total price is SHOWN alongside components, partitioned pricing HURTS preference by ~26.5% (vs +12.5% when the total is hidden) — and modern checkouts, comparison engines, and the new FTC "junk fees" rule (16 CFR Part 464, effective May 2025) increasingly force total-price display, which neutralises or reverses the effect. Partitioning also hurts when surcharges are atypical for the category (-44%), when surcharge benefit is low e.g. plain shipping/handling (-2%, i.e. ~nil), and for hedonic products (-7%). (3) The 1998 mechanism (anchor on base, under-process surcharge -> underestimate total) is actually corroborated by drip-pricing field/lab work (Rhodes 2023 survey; the FTC 2012 Economics of Drip Pricing program), which consistently shows consumers underestimate dripped totals and overpay — but that same literature treats it as a consumer-welfare harm that disappears once the total is made visible, which is the opposite of a durable seller tactic under transparency. No registered-replication failure was found that nullifies the original lab result outright (so not DEBUNKED); the citation itself is genuine and accurately quoted (Morwitz, Greenleaf & Johnson 1998, JMR 35(4), 453-463; recalled $78.27 partitioned vs $83.90 combined verified against multiple secondary sources). The problem is not fabrication — it is gross over-generalisation of a small, highly conditional effect.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS (effect positive) when ALL of: the total price is NOT shown (only base + surcharge visible); price level is above ~$35 (effect grows with magnitude); the surcharge is TYPICAL for the category (e.g. shipping on mail-order, taxes); the surcharge is seen as offering benefit; the product is UTILITARIAN; the buyer is a low shipping-charge-skeptic. Strongest documented win: high surcharge typicality + high perceived benefit (up to +67% preference). LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER / REVERSES when: the total price is displayed (the norm in modern carts, price-comparison, and under FTC junk-fee/UK CMA all-in display rules) -> -26.5%; surcharge is atypical or feels like a gouge -> -44%; surcharge has low/no perceived benefit (plain handling fees) -> ~0; product is hedonic/experiential -> negative; low price band (<~$35) -> all-inclusive wins; skeptical or fairness-sensitive consumers -> no preference or backlash. Measurement base is US business-student lab samples (considered/auction purchases), not impulse buys and not non-US cultures (self-construal moderates), so cross-cultural and impulse generalisation is unestablished. Net for an e-commerce context: as a conversion tactic this is fragile and ethically/legally exposed — if your checkout shows a running total (it should), the effect is gone or negative; reserve any partitioning for typical, benefit-bearing surcharges (genuine shipping), never hide a mandatory fee from the displayed total.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Three live side effects beyond conversion: (1) Legal/compliance — bare partitioning that hides a MANDATORY fee from the headline total is now squarely "drip pricing"/"junk fees": FTC Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (16 CFR 464, eff. May 2025) and UK CMA/DMCCA all-in pricing enforcement require the total mandatory price up front. This is no longer just an ethics caution (the meta-analysis authors warned about deceptive tactics) — it is a regulatory risk. (2) Trust/fairness backlash — surcharges perceived as atypical or unfair (resort fees, surprise handling charges) damage retailer and brand attitudes and amplify dissatisfaction after any service failure. (3) The recall benefit is a consumer HARM (systematic underpayment estimation), so it erodes the moment transparency is imposed and can convert into post-purchase regret/returns.


## Friction, Forms & Motivation

### Fitts's law (target size and distance govern selection time)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Time to acquire a target is a logarithmic function of the distance to it divided by its size (MT = a + b·log2(2A/W)): bigger and closer targets are faster and more reliable to hit. This is one of the most robustly replicated laws in human-computer interaction, holding across decades and input devices.

**Apply:** Make primary CTAs and tap targets large and place them near the user's likely cursor/thumb position; on mobile, size touch targets to ~44px+ and keep them in the thumb zone; use screen edges and corners (effectively infinite width) for high-frequency controls. Larger, closer, well-spaced primary buttons reduce errors and friction at the decisive click.

**Evidence:** Paul M. Fitts (1954), *The information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement*, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 47(6), 381-391 — N: Original reciprocal-tapping experiments with small participant groups (tens of subjects across conditions); validated by hundreds of subsequent HCI replications. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0055392

**Study context:** Original: adult participants in motor-control tapping tasks; modern validation: broad HCI/UX user populations across devices; Lab (original psychomotor experiments); extensively replicated in applied HCI field/lab settings (e.g. NN/g); Not purchase-specific; applies to any pointing/tapping interaction, including buy-button and CTA acquisition

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No failed replication or null meta-analysis exists — the core regularity (bigger + closer targets are acquired faster, scaling with log of distance/size) is one of the best-replicated findings in HCI. The disconfirming evidence is narrower and is about precision, not existence: (1) MacKenzie's 27-year review (IJHCS 2004) found throughput for the SAME mouse-pointing task ranged 2.55–12.5 bps across studies (~5x variation) because of inconsistent ID formulations (Fitts vs Welford vs Shannon), accuracy-adjustment choices, and throughput-calculation methods — so the *coefficients* are not portable even though the *shape* holds; ISO 9241-9 standardisation collapses this to ~3.7–4.9 bps. (2) Documented boundary violations: Fitts's law does not apply to saccadic eye movements; breaks down below ~350 ms movements where continuous visual control is abandoned; array/placeholder studies show last-in-array targets acquired faster than the equation predicts; and width-manipulation vs amplitude-manipulation MT/ID slopes differ, contradicting Fitts's unitary-ID assumption. (3) Theory-level dispute: Rioul & Guiard (Télécom Paris, 2012) show the log-vs-power-model question is mathematically unresolved and Meyer's submovement model implies a *quasi*-logarithmic (not power) relationship — a critique of WHY the log form holds, not evidence it fails. (4) Minor mis-statement in the claim: MT = a + b·log2(2A/W) is the Welford/Fitts-original form; the modern HCI standard is MacKenzie's Shannon form log2(A/W + 1), which fits better and avoids negative ID.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: rapid, visually-guided, goal-directed pointing/tapping with a single discrete target — mouse, trackpad, stylus, and finger-on-touchscreen target acquisition. This squarely covers the UX use case in the claim (sizing and positioning buy-buttons/CTAs: a larger, closer, well-spaced primary action is faster and lower-error to hit; edge/corner placement exploits the "infinite width" of screen borders). Not purchase-type or price-band or culture dependent — it is a motor/perceptual constant, so impulse vs considered purchase is irrelevant to the law itself. DOES NOT transfer cleanly to: saccadic eye-gaze selection (different mechanism); very fast (<~350 ms) ballistic movements where online visual correction is dropped; non-circular/elongated 2D targets (needs the more complex bivariate refinement, not the scalar 1D law); tasks where distance and width are both varied over a wide range (predictive power degrades); and crowded UIs where neighbouring-target/placeholder effects perturb timing. Practically for design: the law tells you direction (bigger+closer+edge = faster) reliably, but absolute time predictions need calibration to the specific device/population and break down for tiny targets at high index of difficulty (high error rate — confirmed in the 13.5"/28"/69.5" display study where the smallest size drove high errors).

**Side-effects / trust cost:** No behavioural/ethical side effects — Fitts's law is an ergonomic regularity, not a persuasion mechanism, so it carries none of the dark-pattern risk of psychological-influence claims. The only "side effect" is mis-application: treating it as a precise predictive constant (it isn't — coefficients vary 5x across studies) or over-shrinking targets to fit layout, which raises error rate at high index of difficulty. Legitimate UX use (larger/closer/edge-anchored primary actions, adequate spacing, comfortably-sized tap targets ~44px+) improves usability for all users including motor-impaired — the opposite of a dark pattern.

### Goal-gradient effect (purchase acceleration near a reward)  `FIELD-VALIDATED`

**Claim:** People accelerate their effort and reduce drop-off as they get visibly closer to a reward threshold; cafe loyalty-card customers bought coffee progressively faster the nearer they were to a free drink, and online raters rated more songs per visit and quit less often as they approached the incentive.

**Apply:** Show users a visible progress meter toward a concrete reward or completion (loyalty stamps, '3 of 5 steps', cart-to-free-shipping bar). Place the user partway up the gradient and make remaining distance small and explicit so the finish line pulls them forward; expect engagement to spike most in the final stretch before the threshold.

**Evidence:** Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky, Yuhuang Zheng (2006), *The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected: Purchase Acceleration, Illusionary Goal Progress, and Customer Retention*, Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39-58 — N: Cafe field study tracked ~900 loyalty cards / ~thousands of coffee purchases; online study used a song-rating site with hundreds of registered raters across thousands of rating sessions (exact N reported per study in the paper). https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39

**Study context:** Real cafe loyalty-program customers (US) and registered users of an online music-rating website; Field experiments + analysis of real transaction/behavioural logs (not lab); Low-cost repeat purchase (coffee, ~$1-3 band) and effort-based non-monetary engagement (rating songs)

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No failed replication, no meta-analysis zeroing the effect, and no published critique claiming it's a pure artifact — so nothing kills it. The genuine disconfirming/limiting evidence: (1) Effect size is MODEST, not dramatic — the headline cafe finding is only ~20% interpurchase-time reduction (~0.7 days) and ~16% faster card completion, at t=2.6, p<.05 (borderline significance). (2) Moderator that ATTENUATES it: "Power reduces the goal gradient effect" (Garbinsky et al., J. Experimental Social Psychology 2019) — high-power individuals show little proximity-driven acceleration; the effect is strongest in low-power states. (3) A 2025 restaurant-industry field study (Ko & Song, Cornell Hospitality Quarterly / SAGE) found NONLINEAR reward-gradient behavior, qualifying the simple monotonic acceleration story. (4) The "post-reward dip / reset" (Kivetz et al. themselves; Drèze & Nunes) means acceleration is not sustained — motivation drops to baseline after the reward is hit before re-accelerating, so the effect is cyclical not cumulative. (5) The authors' own admission in the paper: prior to 2006 "there is no published study that provides unequivocal evidence of a systematic, behavioral goal gradient in humans" — the human evidence base was thin, mostly extrapolated from 1930s-40s rat-runway physiology (Hull 1934, Brown 1948).

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS where measured: low-cost, high-frequency repeat purchases (coffee ~$1-3 band) and low-stakes effort-based engagement (rating songs online), among US loyalty-program members, with VISIBLE incremental progress toward a near, attainable, discrete threshold. Mechanism is proportional goal distance (effort scales with fraction of distance remaining), so it needs a clearly displayed progress indicator and a goal close enough to feel reachable. LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER cleanly to: (a) considered/high-ticket one-off purchases (cars, B2B, property) where deliberation dominates and there's no repeat-purchase cadence to accelerate; (b) high-power decision-makers (power attenuates the effect per Garbinsky 2019); (c) goals that are far away or feel unattainable (the gradient is shallow far from the threshold — drop-off, not acceleration); (d) sustained motivation claims — post-reward reset means each cycle restarts, so it does not compound. Cross-culturally untested at scale (original data US-only); progress must be salient — a hidden points balance won't trigger it.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Largely benign and non-manipulative IF the threshold and progress are honestly displayed. Two cautions: (1) The "illusionary/endowed progress" lever (giving fake starter stamps, e.g. a 12-stamp card with 2 pre-filled vs a 10-stamp card) works by manufacturing a sense of progress — it is effective but edges toward a dark pattern if the "head start" is presented as a gift the customer earned rather than reframing. Use the honest version (reframe the same requirement as "already started") not deceptive inflation. (2) Post-reward dip is a real retention risk: customers slow down right after redeeming, so a single-tier program can see churn at the reset point — design recurring/visible next goals to bridge it. No evidence of harm to users; the risk is to the business (over-investing in a modest effect) and to trust (if progress framing is deceptive).

### Endowed progress effect (artificial head-start toward a goal)  `FIELD-VALIDATED`

**Claim:** Giving people artificial advancement toward a goal raises completion rates versus an equivalent fresh start: car-wash customers given a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled redeemed at 34% vs 19% for those given a blank 8-stamp card requiring the identical 8 purchases, and they completed faster.

**Apply:** Never start a multi-step form, onboarding, or loyalty flow at 0%. Frame it as already begun: a progress bar that opens at step 1-of-N already complete, a profile that's '20% set up' on signup, or a punch card with the first slots pre-stamped. Crucially, give a (even arbitrary) reason for the head-start - 'because you signed up today' - or the effect collapses.

**Evidence:** Joseph C. Nunes, Xavier Drèze (2006), *The Endowed Progress Effect: How Artificial Advancement Increases Effort*, Journal of Consumer Research, 32(4), 504-512 — N: Car-wash field experiment with 300 loyalty-card customers. https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/32/4/504/1796577

**Study context:** Real car-wash customers (US) on a loyalty program; Field experiment (loyalty cards handed out in situ), backed by supporting lab/survey studies in the same paper; Repeat service purchase, mid-low price band (single car wash)

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No failed replication, registered-report null, or meta-analysis finding ~zero effect surfaced after targeted adversarial search — the effect is not on any replication-crisis hit-list. The strongest disconfirming material is not a debunk but a set of BOUNDARY conditions: (1) the original paper itself reports the effect weakens/disappears when no reason is given for the head start — an arbitrary justification ("special promotion") suffices, but with zero justification the motivational lift collapses; (2) Bagchi & Li (2011, JCR, "Illusionary Progress in Loyalty Programs") show the effect is moderated by reward distance and step-size ambiguity, i.e. it is sensitive to how progress magnitudes are framed and can be diluted; (3) endowment delivered late in goal pursuit can devalue prior personal effort and reduce loyalty rather than help. Methodological caveat against the headline specifically: the 34% vs 19% car-wash result is a single-site field experiment (one car wash) with the lab/survey studies in the same paper as support — there is no large-N independent direct replication of that exact field design.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it holds: repeat, low-to-mid-price, frequency-reward purchases on a loyalty/punch-card structure (the car wash and café contexts where it was actually measured — US samples), where a (even arbitrary) reason for the head start is given and the endowment is granted early in the goal sequence. Where it likely does NOT transfer: (1) head starts with NO stated justification — the lift disappears (author-stated moderator); (2) late-stage endowment near goal completion — can backfire by devaluing the customer's own prior effort; (3) one-off considered/high-price purchases (cars, B2B, big-ticket) where a single reward goal isn't the choice driver — untested and mechanistically weaker; (4) settings where the manipulation is transparently a gimmick or where step-size/reward-distance framing makes the "progress" feel hollow (Bagchi & Li moderators). Cross-culturally untested beyond US consumer samples; treat non-Western/non-loyalty contexts as unverified.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Two main risks. First, perceived-manipulation backlash: if the head start reads as a transparent trick rather than a credited bonus, the effect can null out (the "reason for endowment" moderator) — always pair the head start with a stated, even minor, justification. Second, effort-devaluation: endowing progress too late, or repeatedly resetting goals after a reward, can demotivate (the post-reward dip Kivetz et al. document). Not a dark pattern when the goal and reward are real and honestly disclosed; it tips into one only if the "extra" progress is illusory in a way that misleads the customer about what they actually have to spend.

### Form-field minimization (reducing checkout/form friction)  `FIELD-VALIDATED`

**Claim:** Excess form fields are a leading driver of checkout/form abandonment; Baymard finds the average checkout has ~11-13 fields while most can be done in ~8, and that field count and per-field friction (e.g. 30% of users stall at 'Address Line 2', 42% mistype 'Full Name' into a 'First Name' field) materially raise abandonment, with checkout-process complexity cited by ~22-26% of cart abandoners.

**Apply:** Cut every non-essential field; default to one 'Full Name' field over First/Last, drop 'Address Line 2', auto-derive city/state from postcode, and avoid optional fields that add visual length. Fewer fields and fewer decisions per screen lower the perceived and actual effort of completing the form, directly reducing abandonment.

**Evidence:** Baymard Institute (Christian Holst et al.) (2024), *Checkout Flow Average Form Fields / E-Commerce Checkout Usability research*, Baymard Institute (original large-scale UX research; 25+ rounds of moderated usability testing, 4,400+ test sessions, 214-site benchmark) — N: Qualitative: 25+ rounds, 4,400+ moderated test/site sessions (think-aloud); Quantitative benchmark: 214 top US/EU e-commerce sites; abandonment meta-stat aggregates 50 studies (avg 70.2% cart abandonment). https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields

**Study context:** US/EU online shoppers in moderated usability tests + top-grossing e-commerce checkout flows; Field/applied UX research - moderated lab usability testing plus real-site benchmarking (counts as field evidence per task brief); Considered and impulse e-commerce purchases across price bands; checkout context

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The strongest disconfirming evidence attacks a NAIVE causal version of the claim ("fewer fields always = more conversions"), which is well-documented as false: (1) Michael Aagaard / Unbounce cut a sign-up form 9→6 fields and conversions DROPPED 14%; keeping 9 fields but improving label clarity then LIFTED them 19% — field count was not the lever, relevance/copy was. (2) Unbounce's own landing-page database showed conversion highest at 1 field, leveling 4-7, then CLIMBING again — 10 fields out-converting 3, breaking any monotonic "fewer is better" relationship. (3) CXL (a respected CRO authority) concludes the "reduce form fields" rule is overstated and that field RELEVANCE, not raw count, drives results. Crucially, though, all these counterexamples are LEAD-GEN / landing-page forms, NOT e-commerce checkout — a different context where removed fields can strip out questions users actually wanted to answer. The Baymard claim is scoped to checkout, which sidesteps the bulk of this critique. Two further caveats on the claim's wording: (a) Baymard's own language is correlational/best-practice ("as the number of form fields increases, checkout UX performance decreases"), NOT a controlled causal estimate; there is no RCT/meta-analysis establishing a clean per-field abandonment elasticity. (b) The "~22-26%" abandonment figure conflates two ADJACENT Baymard reasons — "too long/complicated checkout" (22%) and "forced account creation" (26%) — and the specific source blog page itself states 17% for checkout complexity, so the precise number is loosely cited though directionally correct. No meta-analysis or registered replication finds the underlying effect to be near-zero in checkout contexts.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: e-commerce CHECKOUT flows, where purchase intent already exists — removing redundant fields (Address Line 2 buried, Company, separate phone, duplicate name fields) and fixing per-field friction reliably reduces effort and friction-driven abandonment. Strongest for mobile (typing cost highest, autofill payoff largest), US/EU shoppers (the tested population), and standard physical-goods checkout across price bands. The per-field-friction findings (Address Line 2 stall, Full Name mis-parse) transfer broadly because they are about field DESIGN, not count. WEAKER / LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: (1) LEAD-GEN and landing-page forms, where added fields can be engaging or signal value and where cutting them can flood pipelines with low-quality leads — here field count is explicitly NOT a reliable conversion lever (the published counterexamples live here). (2) The naive prescription "just cut fields to raise conversion" — multiple controlled tests show clarity/copy/relevance can outweigh count, and over-trimming can hurt. (3) High-consideration or trust-sensitive purchases (finance, insurance, B2B) where users may EXPECT thorough forms and where a too-short form can reduce perceived legitimacy or downstream qualification. (4) Markets/cultures outside the US/EU test base (address formats, payment norms, name-field conventions differ) — the specific field-friction percentages may not replicate. (5) The claim is about MARGINAL friction within an otherwise good flow; it does not override the dominant abandonment drivers Baymard itself ranks higher — unexpected extra/shipping costs (~48%) and forced account creation (~26%) — which are not field-count problems.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Over-aggressive field cutting has documented downsides: in lead-gen it raises volume but lowers lead quality (e.g. 3-field forms convert ~3x higher than 9-field but yield less-qualified leads); in checkout, removing fields that capture genuinely needed data (correct address components, fraud/AVS signals, phone for delivery) can shift cost downstream to failed deliveries, fraud, or support load rather than eliminating it. The safer, better-evidenced move is reducing PERCEIVED and unnecessary friction (autofill, smart defaults, hiding/auto-detecting Address Line 2, single Full Name field, deferring optional fields) rather than blindly minimizing count. No dark-pattern risk in the core practice; risk is purely operational (data you needed but stopped collecting).

### Labor illusion / operational transparency  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Signalling that work is being done on the user's behalf raises perceived value and satisfaction even when the outcome is identical; across five experiments (travel and dating sites) users sometimes preferred a site that showed visible 'searching...' effort and made them wait over one returning instant identical results, via perceived effort and reciprocity.

**Apply:** When a process is genuinely doing work (search, matching, calculation, compiling results), surface it - animated step-by-step status ('Searching 140 airlines... checking 12 sites...') instead of an instant or opaque spinner. The visible labor makes the result feel more thorough and valuable and the wait feel justified.

**Evidence:** Ryan W. Buell, Michael I. Norton (2011), *The Labor Illusion: How Operational Transparency Increases Perceived Value*, Management Science, 57(9), 1564-1579 — N: Five experiments simulating online travel and online dating services (lab/online, per-experiment N reported in paper; hundreds of participants total). https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376

**Study context:** Online experiment participants using simulated travel-search and dating-match services; Controlled lab/online experiments (simulated services), not live commercial field data; Information-service value perception (no direct purchase); transfers to search/results-heavy product flows

**Disconfirming evidence found:** No direct independent replication of Buell & Norton (2011) was located — neither confirming nor refuting the five-experiment original, and no meta-analysis of this specific effect exists. The strongest disconfirming signal is at the mechanism level: the cognate "effort heuristic" (Kruger, Wirtz, Van Boven & Altermatt 2004) — the same "visible effort raises valuation" logic Buell-Norton rely on — was subjected to a preregistered high-N replication (N approx 1405, MTurk + Prolific). It replicated Experiment 2 but FAILED Experiment 1; a revised attempt found the effort effect for liking/quality but NOT for monetary value. Since Buell-Norton's headline outcome is precisely perceived VALUE/willingness-to-pay, the part of the chain that wobbled in replication is the part the claim leans on. Buell's own later work plus applied evidence document robust reversal conditions (below). One secondary source (Medium) also misattributes the term to "Buell, Kim & Tsay" — the correct attribution is Buell & Norton 2011, so the folklore version of the claim is already drifting.

**Boundary conditions:** Holds: information-service flows where the wait is genuine-feeling, the effort is plausibly NECESSARY to a quality result, the outcome meets or beats expectations, and the user attributes the work TO them (engaged/appreciated, not surveilled). Measured in US online-experiment populations on search/matching tasks with no real money or stakes — a value-PERCEPTION not a purchase. Likely does NOT transfer: (1) when effort looks wasteful, inefficient, or theatrical — value gain shrinks or inverts; (2) when the service is suboptimal/harmful — transparency exposes the deficiency and backfires (Buell); (3) when the wait is long enough to cross the curvilinear peak — impatience dominates; (4) impulse / high-time-pressure flows where speed is the value proposition (instant-results expectation) — exposing time cost reduced completion in Irrational Labs field tests; (5) long/empty progress bars or slow early progress, which signal remaining effort and depress completion; (6) deceptive fake loaders — undocumented for trust effects but a clear dark-pattern risk if users detect the manufactured delay. The monetary-WTP transfer is the least safe extrapolation given the cognate effort-heuristic replication failed on exactly that outcome.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Dark-pattern and trust risk if the labor is manufactured (fake/artificially-slowed loaders): the original effect rests on perceived GENUINE effort and reciprocity, so a user who detects a fabricated delay may swing to distrust — a downside the 2011 lab studies did not test. Backfire is documented when transparency makes users feel watched rather than served, when it reveals a bad process, or when exposed time/effort cost dominates the perceived benefit. Use only where the work shown is real and the wait is honest.


## Scarcity, Urgency & Loss

### Attribute framing (positive vs negative valence framing of the same fact)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Describing the identical attribute in positive valence terms ('95% success', '90% fat-free') produces more favourable evaluations than the logically equivalent negative frame ('5% failure', '10% fat') — a robust, replicated framing effect.

**Apply:** Frame numeric claims, guarantees and risk language in the positive valence: '95% of customers renew' over '5% cancel'; 'works on 9 of 10 devices' over '1 in 10 unsupported'; emphasise what the user keeps/gains. Use for product specs, social-proof stats, guarantee/risk copy, and pricing comparisons.

**Evidence:** Irwin P. Levin, Sandra L. Schneider, Gary J. Gaeth (1998), *All Frames Are Not Created Equal: A Typology and Critical Analysis of Framing Effects*, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 76(2), 149-188 — N: Review/typology synthesising dozens of attribute-framing studies (foundational case: Levin & Gaeth 1988, ground-beef '75% lean' vs '25% fat'). https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2804

**Study context:** Aggregated across many studies — student and adult consumer samples; classic beef-labelling study used consumers tasting/rating ground beef; Mostly lab and survey experiments; some applied health-decision field work; Product evaluation and consumer-choice tasks, generally low-to-moderate involvement

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The disconfirming evidence is about MAGNITUDE and BEHAVIOURAL TRANSFER, not existence. (1) Piñon & Gambara (2005) meta-analysis (51 studies, ~13,500 subjects) puts attribute framing at only d=0.26 — the SMALLEST of the three framing types (risky-choice d=0.44, goal d=0.44). Robust in direction, modest in size. (2) The strongest crack: Dolgopolova et al. (2021/2022) food meta-analysis finds the gain-frame effect on consumer ATTITUDES is real but the effect on behavioural INTENTIONS is 'close to zero and insignificant.' So the persuasion-to-purchase leap is NOT supported — it is an evaluation/attitude effect, not a demonstrated buying-behaviour effect. (3) Freling, Vincent & Henard (2014, OBHDP 124(2):95-109, 'When not to accentuate the positive') show the valence-consistent shift is CONDITIONAL — negative frames can be MORE persuasive for psychologically close issues (construal-level x psychological-distance interaction), directly contradicting the secondary-source claim that 'no case exists where a negative frame beats a positive frame.' (4) Effect inflated by student samples (a significant moderator in the food meta-analysis). No large registered/Many-Labs replication is SPECIFIC to attribute framing — the famous Many Labs gain-loss replication (d=0.60) is Tversky-Kahneman RISKY-CHOICE (Asian disease) framing, a different effect, so the strongest replication credential does not actually attach to attribute framing.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: low-to-moderate-involvement product/attribute EVALUATION and attitude-rating tasks (taste, quality, healthiness ratings); lab/survey settings; effect reliably present but small (d~0.26); strongest for a single salient attribute presented dichotomously (lean/fat, success/failure, fat-free/fat); inflated in student samples. LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: (1) actual purchase/behavioural INTENTION — food meta-analysis effect there is near zero, so do not assume a '90% fat-free' label converts browsers to buyers, only that it nudges the attitude rating; (2) psychologically CLOSE / high-construal decisions, where negative frames can out-persuade positive ones (Freling et al. 2014) — e.g. a near-term, personally-relevant, concrete decision; (3) high-involvement / considered purchases where evaluators scrutinise the numeric content and the frame washes out; (4) numerically sophisticated audiences who spontaneously translate '95% success' <-> '5% failure'; (5) emotionally-engaged consumers (Poor et al. 2023 found focusing on emotion during consumption attenuates the effect). Cross-culture generalisability is largely untested in the located meta-analyses (predominantly Western/US samples). Practical read for a designer: frame positively as a cheap default for attribute/benefit copy and rating-type signals; do NOT bank on it as a standalone conversion driver, and avoid it for concrete, high-stakes, near-term decisions where a candid negative frame may land better.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Low manipulation risk — this is not a dark pattern when the underlying fact is identical and truthful ('95% success' = '5% failure'). The integrity line is FALSE EQUIVALENCE: '90% fat-free' is honest; selectively framing only the flattering attribute while burying a material negative is not. Side effect to watch: sophisticated or sceptical audiences may read aggressively positive framing as spin, eroding trust (no direct study located, flagged as inference). For health/safety or financial decisions where the recipient is psychologically close to the outcome, a positive frame can be LESS effective and arguably less ethical than candid disclosure — see boundary condition 2.

### Scarcity effect (limited supply raises perceived value)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** When a product's available supply is low (or framed as low), people rate it as more valuable and desirable than an identical abundant product; the effect is strongest when supply visibly drops from abundant to scarce and when shoppers do not detect deliberate marketer manipulation.

**Apply:** Show a genuine, accurate low-stock indicator on the product/cart page ('Only 3 left'), or display a real limited-edition / limited-run badge. Tie supply-based scarcity to experiential and luxury items where it tests strongest; keep numbers truthful and product-specific (not a sitewide constant) so they don't read as fabricated.

**Evidence:** Stephen Worchel, Jerry Lee, Akanbi Adewole (1975), *Effects of supply and demand on ratings of object value*, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 32(5), 906-914 — N: 200 female undergraduates across 2 experiments (cookie-jar lab study). https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1976-22072-001

**Study context:** Female undergraduate psychology students (University of North Carolina); lab experiment (cookies in jars rated for value); field/market generalisation comes from later replications and the Barton et al. 2022 meta-analysis; Original study was a hypothetical consumable rating, not a real purchase; meta-analytic evidence spans real and intended purchases across price bands

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The claim's specific mechanism — restricted SUPPLY ("limited supply raises value") — is the WEAKEST form of scarcity in the most granular meta-analysis. Ladeira et al. 2023 (Psychology & Marketing, 335 effect sizes): excessive-DEMAND scarcity SMD = 0.5484 (95% CI 0.441–0.656) vs restricted-SUPPLY scarcity SMD = 0.1859 (95% CI 0.136–0.236) — supply scarcity is ~3x weaker and the moderator contrast is significant (β = -0.346, p < .001). The overall pooled scarcity effect is small: d = 0.2776 (95% CI 0.224–0.321), with high heterogeneity (I² implied by Q(224)=875.83, p<.001). Lynn's 1991 commodity-theory meta-analysis already characterised scarcity effects as "small in magnitude." So the headline "limited supply raises perceived value" overstates a mechanism that is real but modest and the weakest of the scarcity family. Separately, scarcity reliably BACKFIRES when manipulation is detected: Biraglia et al. 2021 (scarcity appeals trigger consumer anger + brand-switching), the "scarce-insincere inference" (2025, Journal of Retailing), and persuasion-knowledge/reactance literature all show reversal — consistent with the claim's own caveat but undercutting it as a default tactic. No DEBUNKING (no registered-replication failure; effect is non-zero and directionally consistent across three meta-analyses), so not DEBUNKED; but the mixed magnitude picture + supply being the weak mechanism + documented backfire = CONTESTED, not REPLICATED.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS (strongest): demand-driven scarcity ("selling fast", "high demand", sold-out popularity) on utilitarian products; enduring luxuries with social-signaling value; products with seasonality. Ladeira 2023 moderators — enduring > transitory luxuries; social signaling present > absent; variety scarcity > category scarcity. Barton 2022 — demand-scarcity best for utilitarian goods, supply-scarcity best for experiences, time-scarcity for high-involvement. HOLDS WEAKLY: pure restricted-supply framing ("only 5 left", "limited stock") — small effect (SMD ~0.19), the literal subject of this claim. NULL/NOT SUPPORTED: urgency framing (limited-quantity vs limited-time made no reliable difference, p = .11, ns). LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER / REVERSES: when shoppers infer the scarcity is engineered/insincere (persuasion knowledge + reactance) → anger and brand-switching (the claim's own "no detected manipulation" caveat is load-bearing and well-evidenced); repeated exposure erodes it via skepticism. Population caveat: original was female US psychology undergrads rating a hypothetical consumable, not a real purchase; field generalisation rests on the later meta-analyses (real + intended purchases, multiple cultures), so treat the cookie study as illustrative, not as field proof. Price band: effect is amplified for expensive/luxury/exclusive goods (assumed-expensiveness mediation), weaker for low-cost commodities.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Real backfire risk, not just theoretical. (1) Reactance/anger: scarcity appeals that fail to deliver (item sells out) raise consumer anger and intention to buy from a competitor (Biraglia et al. 2021). (2) Scarce-insincere inference: low advertised quantities can read as manipulative/pushy, lowering perceived retailer sincerity and purchase intent (2025 Journal of Retailing). (3) Persuasion-knowledge activation: detected manipulative intent triggers skepticism and brand-trust damage; effect erodes with repeated exposure to artificial scarcity. Net: deploy only with GENUINE scarcity and transparent reason ("handmade batch of 50", real stock counts) — fake "only 3 left" timers risk reputational harm and legal exposure (UK CPRs / misleading-availability rules). Avoid on low-trust audiences or for staple/utilitarian low-cost items where it reads as pressure.

### Urgency / countdown timers (time-based scarcity) — with documented backfire  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Time-limited deadlines and countdown timers can accelerate decisions and lift conversion for high-involvement purchases, but time-based 'urgency' scarcity is the weakest and least reliable scarcity type, and fake or arbitrary timers/deadlines are a documented deceptive dark pattern that damages trust when detected.

**Apply:** Use a countdown timer ONLY when the deadline is real and the price/offer genuinely reverts when the clock hits zero (real sale window, real dispatch cutoff like 'order in 2h for next-day delivery'). Never reset the timer on page reload and never run a sitewide perpetual timer. Reserve it for considered/high-involvement purchases where a deadline aids a real decision; avoid stacking it on every page (urgency fatigue).

**Evidence:** Arunesh Mathur, Gunes Acar, Michael J. Friedman, Elena Lucherini, Jonathan Mayer, Marshini Chetty, Arvind Narayanan (2019), *Dark Patterns at Scale: Findings from a Crawl of 11K Shopping Websites*, Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction (CSCW) — N: ~53,000 product pages across ~11,000 shopping sites; 1,818 dark-pattern instances on 1,254 sites (~11.1%). https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07032

**Study context:** The ~11,000 most-popular shopping websites (Alexa ranking), real commercial e-commerce; online field audit / automated crawl plus manual classification; Real e-commerce across all price bands; urgency/scarcity patterns observed mostly on impulse-to-considered retail goods

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The strongest available meta-analysis directly undercuts the "urgency lifts purchase" half of the claim for time-based scarcity specifically. Ladeira et al. (2023, Psychology & Marketing, 10.1002/mar.21816) consolidated a large corpus (heterogeneity χ²(224)=875.83, p<.001) and found product scarcity overall has a positive effect on purchase decision (β=.2776), BUT among scarcity-cue moderators, URGENCY was the ONLY one that failed: the limited-quantity-vs-limited-time contrast was non-significant (β=0.1030, SE=0.0649, 95% CI −0.0242 to 0.2302, z=1.5866, p=0.1126 ns) — H4 NOT supported. By contrast economic scarcity (excess-demand > restricted-supply, β=−.346, p<.001) and taxonomic (variety > category, β=.261, p<.001) both moderated strongly. Limited-time scarcity standalone is only modest (k=51, SMD=0.3952; limited-quantity k=96, SMD=0.287) with no significant difference between them. So the authoritative quantitative synthesis says time-based urgency is the weakest/least-distinguishable scarcity lever — meaning the practical "lift conversion" benefit, where it exists, comes from generic scarcity, not from the time mechanism per se. Independent experimental literature agrees: limited-quantity consistently beats limited-time because time-only scarcity lacks inter-buyer competition (Aggarwal et al. 2011; Wu et al. 2021 'limited time or limited quantity'). Separately, the cited Mathur et al. (2019) paper does NOT itself measure effectiveness or backfire — it documents prevalence/deception only (157 deceptive countdown timers on 140 of 11K sites that reset on reload), so the single citation under-supports the multi-part claim; the 'weakest scarcity' and 'backfire' legs rest on other sources (Ladeira 2023; Tiemessen 2022).

**Boundary conditions:** Where it likely HOLDS: time-based urgency adds most where it stacks with an actual price incentive (a genuine limited-time discount, not a bare clock) and on higher-involvement/considered purchases where a deadline overcomes deliberation paralysis (Ladeira's secondary note that time-based scarcity suits high-involvement goods; Tiemessen's effect appeared with discount + timer, not timer alone). Measured populations: Western online shoppers (US/EU/NL lab samples ages 19–53; Mathur's crawl was US-Alexa-ranked English-language e-commerce). Older consumers were more susceptible (Kim 2023). Where it LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: (1) bare arbitrary countdowns with no real deadline or discount — these carry the weakest effect and the highest detection/backfire risk; (2) repeat or sophisticated visitors who have learned timers are fake (resetting-on-reload is now widely recognised → skepticism, reactance, abandonment); (3) low-involvement impulse buys where deliberation isn't the bottleneck, so a deadline adds little; (4) the urgency *mechanism* itself does not reliably outperform other scarcity types — substituting a low-stock/quantity cue is more reliable than a timer because it triggers inter-buyer competition that pure time scarcity lacks; (5) trust-sensitive / repeat-relationship contexts (subscriptions, B2B, premium brands) where a detected fake timer damages long-term loyalty more than it lifts one conversion; (6) jurisdictions actively enforcing against deceptive timers (EU UCPD, Dutch ACM, FTC) where fake countdowns are now an enforcement target, not just a reputational risk.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Documented backfire is real and bidirectional. (1) Detection-driven distrust: when a timer is seen to reset on reload or never expire, consumers categorise the seller as deceptive — Tiemessen (2022) measured this directly: deceptive timers made consumers 'skeptical and averse,' diminishing the pull of even a genuine discount on the same page. (2) Reactance / perceived manipulation: a subset abandon specifically because urgency feels pushy/anxiety-inducing even when they wanted the product. (3) Urgency fatigue: when every page has a timer, shoppers learn to ignore all of them, eroding the tactic store-wide. (4) Legal/regulatory: fake or arbitrary timers are a named deceptive ('dark') pattern under EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and FTC scrutiny; the Dutch regulator ACM has formally confronted retailers using misleading countdowns — so the downside is now enforcement exposure, not merely lost trust. Practical upshot for non-dark use: only deploy timers tied to a REAL deadline/discount, never reset them, and prefer a genuine low-stock/quantity cue (stronger and lower-risk) over a bare clock.

### Endowment effect (ownership / trial-induced ownership raises valuation)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Once people feel they own (or possess) an item, they value it markedly more than identical non-owners — in the canonical experiment, median willingness-to-accept to sell was roughly twice willingness-to-pay to buy (~2:1).

**Apply:** Engineer a sense of pre-purchase ownership: free trials and freemium periods, free no-questions returns, 'your cart / your plan / your design' possessive framing, personalisation and configurators that let the user build/customise the product before buying. The felt ownership during a trial raises reluctance to give the product up at conversion/renewal.

**Evidence:** Daniel Kahneman, Jack L. Knetsch, Richard H. Thaler (1990), *Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem*, Journal of Political Economy, 98(6), 1325-1348 — N: Cornell undergraduate cohorts in repeated mug/token market trials (median selling price ~$7 vs buying ~$3). https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/261737

**Study context:** Cornell University undergraduate students; lab market experiment (coffee mugs and induced-value tokens traded in repeated markets); Low-value consumer good (~$6 mug); robustness to high-stakes and substitutable/fungible goods is contested

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The canonical ~2:1 mug result replicates as a raw WTA/WTP gap, but its CAUSAL interpretation (ownership raises valuation) is challenged by serious published work. Plott & Zeiler (AER 2005, "The WTP-WTA Gap, the 'Endowment Effect', Subject Misconceptions...") and (AER 2007, "Exchange Asymmetries Incorrectly Interpreted...") show the gap DISAPPEARS when procedures control for subject misconceptions about the elicitation mechanism (anonymity, BDM training, neutral "the object is yours" framing, equidistant placement) — and reappears under traditional procedures. They argue the effect is a methodological artifact, not a preference. Klass & Zeiler push further: endowment THEORY is "wrong" / mislabeled. Counter-evidence cuts both ways: Fehr, Hakimov & Kübler (Eur. Econ. Rev. 2015) FAILED to replicate Plott & Zeiler's null (the gap survived their controls), so the artifact claim is itself contested. Tunçel & Hammitt (J. Env. Econ. Mgmt 2014) meta-analysis: WTA/WTP ratios across studies range from <5 to >100, with the disparity significantly SMALLER for private goods, student subjects, real-world experience, and incentive-compatible mechanisms — i.e. the headline ~2:1 shrinks sharply under better methods and is largest precisely for hard-to-value public/environmental goods. Boundary failures: Apicella et al. (AER 2014) found NO endowment effect in isolated Hadza hunter-gatherers (only market-exposed Hadza showed it); Maddux et al. (2010) found it attenuated in East Asians and reversible by collectivism priming; Shogren et al. found little effect for substitutable/fungible goods. So: the phenomenon is real and robust in WEIRD lab settings with naive subjects and idiosyncratic goods, but the magnitude is method-dependent and the "ownership causes valuation" mechanism is genuinely disputed.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS most reliably: WEIRD (Western, educated) naive subjects; idiosyncratic/non-fungible goods you can't trivially re-buy (mugs, wine, event tickets, real estate, environmental/public goods — where it is LARGEST); one-shot or low-experience valuation; affect-laden or self-relevant items. WEAKENS / LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: fungible/substitutable goods and money-like tokens (induced-value tokens trade at parity in KKT's own control); experienced traders / repeated market exposure (gap shrinks with learning); incentive-compatible elicitation done cleanly (Plott-Zeiler-style controls can erase it); isolated, non-market cultures (Hadza hunter-gatherers show none until market-exposed); East-Asian / collectivist populations (attenuated, priming-reversible). For applied conversion/retail use: a free-trial or "it's yours" framing on a distinctive, hard-to-comparison-shop product in a Western market is the strong case; a commodity/fungible SKU with transparent pricing and an experienced buyer is the weak case. Treat "~2:1" as an upper-band lab figure for idiosyncratic goods, not a universal multiplier.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Trial-to-paid and "make it yours" framing can backfire if the perceived gain feels like a manufactured loss at conversion (reactance / buyer's remorse, refund spikes) — strongest where the good is commoditized and the customer knows the market price, which is exactly where the effect is weakest. Net: low upside + real downside on fungible/transparent-priced items; meaningful upside on distinctive, self-relevant goods.

### Loss-aversion framing (CONTESTED — include with strong caveats)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** The classic claim is that losses loom ~2x larger than equivalent gains, so 'you'll lose X' messaging should beat 'you'll gain X'; this general principle is now seriously contested and loss framing does NOT reliably outperform gain framing.

**Apply:** If used at all, apply narrowly to genuine forfeiture moments where the user already has something to lose: expiring saved cart/discount, lapsing membership benefits, 'don't lose your progress/data' at cancellation. Avoid manufacturing fake 'loss' framing for net-new purchases, and A/B test against a plain gain frame rather than assuming loss framing wins.

**Evidence:** David Gal, Derek D. Rucker (2018), *The Loss of Loss Aversion: Will It Loom Larger Than Its Gain?*, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28(3), 497-516 — N: Theoretical/empirical review re-examining the loss-aversion literature (not a single-sample experiment). https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1047

**Study context:** Review across consumer and decision-making studies; original Tversky & Kahneman 1991 reference-dependence work used student/decision samples; Conceptual review plus re-analysis of existing experiments; Spans hypothetical bets, consumer choices, and framing tasks; effect is highly context-dependent

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Note: this claim is itself the contrarian/critical position (loss framing does NOT reliably beat gain framing), so "evidence against the claim" = evidence that loss framing DOES reliably win. That evidence is weak. (1) Published defenders argue loss aversion is real but moderated: Mrkva et al. 2020 (JCP, "Moderating Loss Aversion: ...reports of its death are greatly exaggerated") shows loss aversion re-emerges at larger stakes (~$100+), with endowment, and with domain expertise; Simonson & Kivetz and Higgins & Liberman published formal rebuttals to Gal & Rucker in the same 2018 JCP issue arguing for "contingent" loss aversion. (2) Loss framing genuinely wins in a few narrow behavioural niches — O'Keefe & Jensen find a small but significant loss-framing advantage for breast-cancer screening (detection behaviour). (3) Marketing "loss framing wins" wins exist but are folklore-grade — e.g. an Optimizely-style blog claim of a loss-framed ad getting "24% higher CTR / 18% more form submits" — single unreplicated A/B anecdotes, no published effect size, exactly the kind of source the claim warns against. None of this restores the "losses loom 2x larger, so use loss framing" rule as a reliable default.

**Boundary conditions:** WHERE LOSS FRAMING MAY STILL HELP (narrow): high-stakes/large-magnitude decisions (~$100+ in lab terms), endowment/status-quo contexts (something already "yours" being taken away — free-trial expiry, losing accumulated points/progress, downgrade warnings), risk-/uncertainty-laden detection-type decisions (the breast-cancer-screening analogue: "find out before it's too late"), and expert/high-involvement audiences. WHERE IT LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER (most web-conversion copy): low-stakes, small-dollar, prevention/acquisition framing ("get X / unlock Y" vs "miss out on X"), routine impulse or considered purchases, and general consumer audiences — here meta-analytic differences are ~r=.03-.04, i.e. effectively a coin-flip, and gain framing is occasionally the (tiny) winner. Magnitude of any framing effect is small even when significant; direction is behaviour-specific, not a universal rule. Culture: evidence base is overwhelmingly Western (US/EU) lab + health-comms samples; cross-cultural generalisation untested. Practical takeaway for conversion copy: do not treat "say they'll LOSE it" as a default win — it is at best a coin-flip vs gain framing for ordinary purchases. Reserve loss/urgency framing for genuine endowment/expiry situations, and A/B test rather than assume; never deploy fabricated scarcity ("losing" something that isn't real) as that is a dark pattern with no replicated upside.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Manufactured loss/scarcity framing (fake "you're about to lose your discount" timers, invented downgrades) is a dark pattern: it can dent trust and, in the UK, risks falling foul of CPRs/DMCCA aggressive-practice and false-urgency rules — and the supposed conversion payoff is exactly the unreplicated part. Genuine loss framing tied to a real endowment (a trial actually expiring, real accrued points) is legitimate but carries anxiety/reactance risk in low-stakes contexts where it is also least effective, so the downside can exceed the (near-zero) upside.


## Memory, Emotion & Experience

### Peak-end rule (duration neglect)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Retrospective evaluation of an experience is predicted almost entirely by the most intense (peak) moment and the final moment, while total duration is largely neglected; a less-intense added ending improves the remembered experience even though it adds more total discomfort.

**Apply:** Design the emotional arc of a flow, not just the average: engineer a clear positive peak (a delight moment, a celebratory success state) and end every flow on a high note. Post-purchase: replace a blank/abrupt confirmation with a warm, rewarding order-confirmation/thank-you screen; end onboarding with a win state and a progress-complete animation rather than dumping the user into an empty dashboard. Front-load friction (long forms) and finish on the easiest, most satisfying step so the remembered experience is better than the moment-by-moment one.

**Evidence:** Donald A. Redelmeier, Joel Katz, Daniel Kahneman (2003), *Memories of colonoscopy: a randomized trial*, Pain, 104(1-2), 187-194 (DOI 10.1016/S0304-3959(03)00003-4) — N: 682 colonoscopy patients (real-time + global retrospective ratings; randomized extended-end arm). https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/media/0678/colonoscopy%202.pdf

**Study context:** Adult medical patients undergoing colonoscopy (clinical population, not students); foundational lab precedent is Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993) cold-pressor study; Field — randomized clinical trial with real-time pain dial ratings and post-procedure global ratings; procedure durations varied 4-69 minutes; Not a purchase; experience-evaluation paradigm. Web analogue is any multi-step experiential flow (checkout, onboarding, support, signup) rather than a single impulse buy

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three real cracks, none fatal to the core but all qualifying the "almost entirely" framing. (1) Redundancy with the simple average: the 2022 meta-analysis (Alaybek, Wang, Dalal et al., OBHDP 170, 174 effect sizes) found the peak-end effect (r=0.581, 95% CI 0.487-0.661) was only "comparable to" the effect of the overall mean of all moments — not dramatically superior. Ariely/Carmon-style work and computational models show peak-end and a heterogeneity-weighted average of all moments often coincide, so peak-end is not provably the *mechanism*, just a good *predictor*. (2) Documented boundary failures: Wikipedia/secondary reviews cite a 2008 study (Kahneman-lineage, PBR) calling peak-end "not an outstandingly good predictor"; Miron-Shatz found it fails for day-length experiences (hotel stays); a meal study and a vacation survey found duration was NOT neglected and pattern/trend mattered more. (3) Format dependence: making duration salient (visual vs numeric presentation) eliminated duration neglect in lab settings — i.e. the neglect is partly an artifact of how info is encoded, not an immutable law. No registered-replication failure and no meta-analysis finding ~zero effect — so nothing approaching DEBUNKED or CONTESTED.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS where: the experience has a clear, bounded beginning and end; affect is measured moment-to-moment then evaluated retrospectively; episodes are relatively short and emotionally salient (medical procedures, painful/pleasant clips, short flows). Asymmetry: for PAINFUL experiences duration neglect is strong and a milder tail genuinely improves the memory; for PLEASANT experiences the peak effect is strong but duration neglect is softer (very long pleasant episodes accrue somewhat more remembered value). LIKELY DOES NOT transfer to: long, multi-episode or day-plus experiences (hotel stays, vacations) where trend/pattern and even duration re-enter; situations where duration is made salient/visual (neglect collapses); evaluations dominated by a single most-memorable moment rather than peak-or-end. Web analogue (checkout/onboarding/support flows): defensible to engineer a calm, resolving final step and avoid a peak-pain moment, BUT do NOT assume duration is free — a longer flow added purely to "improve the ending" is supported only for genuinely short, bounded, affect-laden flows, not for long multi-session journeys. Population caveat: validated on Western adult clinical/student/general samples; no strong cross-cultural moderation evidence either way. Not a purchase-type/price-band effect — it is experience-evaluation, so impulse-vs-considered and price band are not the relevant moderators; flow length and emotional salience are.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Low manipulation risk in the honest form (end flows on a calm, resolved note; avoid leaving users at peak frustration). Misuse risk: using duration neglect to justify deliberately prolonging an unpleasant process (extra steps) on the theory that "the ending is what's remembered" — this adds real friction/cost and only the *memory* improves, not the lived experience; for repeated/long web flows it can backfire. Also do not over-claim it as the causal mechanism (it co-predicts with the simple average), so "just fix the ending" can under-deliver if the whole experience is poor.

### Mere exposure effect (familiarity → preference)  `REPLICATED`

**Claim:** Repeated exposure to a stimulus, provided it is not initially disliked, reliably increases liking and preference for it; pooled effect size r ≈ 0.26 across 200+ studies, with the relationship following an inverted-U (liking rises then declines with overexposure/satiation).

**Apply:** Consistency and repetition build preference: keep brand identity (logo, colour, typography, tone, key imagery) stable and repeat it across every touchpoint and visit, so returning and retargeted visitors trust and prefer it. Retargeting and email cadence exploit this — but space exposures and vary creative to avoid satiation/ad fatigue. Show, don't gate: surface the product/brand early and often (above the fold, social proof logos) so it feels familiar by decision time. Counts as a reason NOT to constantly redesign brand assets.

**Evidence:** Robert F. Bornstein (1989), *Exposure and affect: Overview and meta-analysis of research, 1968-1987*, Psychological Bulletin, 106(2), 265-289 (DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.106.2.265) — N: Meta-analysis of 134 studies / 208 effect contrasts; overall r ≈ 0.26. Origin: Zajonc (1968), J Pers Soc Psychol Monograph. https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1990-00084-001

**Study context:** Aggregated across 200+ studies — students, general adults, and infants; stimuli from nonsense words and ideographs to faces, ads, and consumer products; Predominantly lab experiments (exposure-then-evaluation); effect also demonstrated for real consumer products and advertising; Cross-cutting — affects brand/ad preference for both impulse and considered categories; web analogue is brand-asset consistency, retargeting frequency, and repeated UI familiarity

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Strongest disconfirming evidence is Chow et al. (2024, Psychology and Aging, Vanderbilt): 4 near-identical experiments, 50 younger (18-35) + 50 older (65-80) participants each, found NO mere-exposure effect for faces in either age group — prompting the authors to ask "does the mere-exposure effect really exist?" Faces specifically appear to be a weak/null stimulus class. Separately, multiple studies failed to replicate the effect for SUBLIMINAL/below-recognition-threshold stimuli (Newell & Shanks line; PubMed 8483644), and a competing account argues recognition may mediate liking rather than exposure driving liking directly. These are domain/condition-specific failures, not a wholesale collapse: they puncture the "reliably increases liking" universality claim, not the core effect. There is also an underlying file-drawer/publication-bias concern common to a 1989 meta-analysis — Bornstein himself flagged that effects shrink with longer durations and recognized stimuli, and the BPS write-up notes effect sizes in this literature "have faded or even vanished as data accumulated."

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: novel/neutral or mildly-positive unfamiliar stimuli (nonsense words, ideographs, ads, novel products, abstract shapes); brief exposures; recognition NOT required (often stronger when stimulus is unrecognised); peak liking within ~10-20 exposures. WEAK/NULL or REVERSES: (1) stimuli already DISLIKED at baseline — exposure amplifies the negative ("exposure to people we initially dislike makes us dislike them more"); the claim's own "provided it is not initially disliked" caveat is load-bearing and correct. (2) FACES — 2024 non-replication; treat face-familiarity claims as unsupported. (3) OVEREXPOSURE / satiation — past ~20-35 reps liking plateaus then declines (ad wear-out, song fatigue). (4) Long exposure durations + high-elaboration considered purchases — "countercontrol": more time to scrutinise attenuates the effect, so it transfers best to low-elaboration impulse/affective judgments, not high-involvement considered evaluation. (5) Anxiety (trait + state) reduces it. For the web/brand analogue: supports consistent brand assets and moderate familiarity, but retargeting frequency has a real wear-out ceiling — beyond optimal frequency it flips to irritation/negative brand perception. Most evidence is WEIRD-sample lab work; one cross-cultural replication (Japan + USA) supports generality, but real-world field effect sizes are smaller than lab.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Two side effects worth flagging for applied use. (1) Wear-out / reactance: over-repetition (high retargeting frequency, ad-blasting) crosses the inverted-U peak into satiation and can actively damage brand sentiment — the effect is self-limiting, not monotonic. (2) Polarity amplification: exposure intensifies whatever the starting affect is, so repeating an asset that lands as neutral-to-negative for a segment entrenches dislike rather than warming it. Net: safe and useful for building familiarity with neutral/positive novel brand assets at moderate frequency; not a lever for low-elaboration "trick into liking," and not reliable for faces or for stimuli users already dislike.

### IKEA effect (effort justification / labor leads to love)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** People assign substantially higher value to objects and outcomes they partially created or assembled themselves than to identical items made by others — but only when the labor is completed successfully; failed or undone effort eliminates the premium.

**Apply:** Let users do meaningful, completable work inside the product so they over-value the result: configurators/customisers (build-your-own bundle, personalise the plan, design-your-own), guided onboarding where the user assembles their own workspace/profile, and 'you built this' summaries before checkout. Surface a recap of the effort invested ('Your custom setup: 6 steps, done') at the decision point. Critical: every effortful step must reach a clear success state — a configurator the user can't finish destroys the valuation gain rather than building it.

**Evidence:** Michael I. Norton, Daniel Mochon, Dan Ariely (2012), *The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love*, Journal of Consumer Psychology, 22(3), 453-460 (DOI 10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002) — N: Four experiments (IKEA boxes, origami, Lego); lab/online adult & student samples, ~50-100+ per study; willingness-to-pay and bidding measures. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1777100

**Study context:** US adults and university students across four experiments (origami folders, box assemblers, Lego builders); a 2016 conceptual replication (Marsh, Kanngiesser & Hood) extended it; Lab and online experiments with real assembly tasks and incentivised WTP/bidding (BDM-style and sealed bids); Considered/customisable goods (furniture, configurable products); maps to configurators, customisation, and DIY-completion flows rather than one-click impulse buys

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The 2026 meta-analysis that otherwise supports the effect (Pelled et al., Psychology & Marketing; k=55, N=5,454, d=0.57, p<.005) ALSO detected significant publication bias: Egger's test for funnel asymmetry p<.001 and Begg & Mazumdar's rank-correlation test p=.006 — both significant, indicating small-study/file-drawer effects. The bias-corrected effect size (PET-PEESE / selection-model adjusted) was not extractable from accessible abstract-level sources, so it is unknown whether d=0.57 survives correction intact or shrinks. Separately, the secondary literature explicitly notes "a lack of consensus as to its robustness and generalizability — while many studies yield strong support, others exhibit weak or null effects on product valuation." So: a real, independently replicated effect, but one whose own meta-analysis flags asymmetry and whose study record is mixed. No single high-profile registered replication failure was found; the threat is publication bias + heterogeneity, not a clean null.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS: considered/customisable/DIY-completion goods (furniture, configurable products, assemble-it-yourself flows) where the user successfully COMPLETES a non-trivial, achievable labor task. Measured almost entirely on US adults/university students in lab + online WTP/bidding paradigms; tangible products show stronger effects than digital. DOES NOT HOLD / premium eliminated: (1) failed or incomplete tasks — labor leads to love ONLY on successful completion; building-then-destroying or abandoning mid-build wipes out the premium (this part of the claim is well-supported); (2) tasks too difficult to complete — over-challenging the user backfires into dissatisfaction; (3) likely does NOT transfer to one-click / zero-effort impulse purchases (no labor = no IKEA effect by construction), to passive consumption, or to digital/intangible goods where ownership attachment is weaker. Cross-cultural generalisability is largely untested — evidence base is WEIRD (US/Western). Real-world field validation beyond lab/online WTP is thin; the effect's commercial extrapolation (configurators, mass customization) rests mostly on lab analogues plus the contested meta-analysis.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Designing in mandatory effort to manufacture attachment has a sharp failure mode the research itself flags: if the user cannot COMPLETE the task, the effect inverts into dissatisfaction and lower valuation — a friction/abandonment risk in conversion flows. So any "make them build it" tactic must guarantee an achievable, clearly-completable task; an over-hard configurator or assembly step is a net negative, not neutral. Also note attachment can become a dark-pattern lever (sunk-cost lock-in via accumulated effort) — keep effort genuine and value-adding, not artificial busywork engineered solely to inflate WTP.

### Reciprocity (gift / value-first)  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** An unsolicited small gift or favour triggers a felt obligation to return the gesture, measurably increasing compliance and the size of a subsequent reciprocal action; personalising the gift amplifies the effect beyond its monetary value.

**Apply:** Lead with genuine free value before asking for anything: a useful free tool/calculator/template/audit, a no-email-gate resource, a sample, or unexpected bonus content delivered first — then make the ask (signup, upgrade, referral). Make the gift feel personal and 'extra' rather than a blanket promo (the personalised, sequential mint condition beat the same candy given all at once). Order matters: give, then ask — not ask, then reward.

**Evidence:** David B. Strohmetz, Bruce Rind, Reed Fisher, Michael Lynn (2002), *Sweetening the till: The use of candy to increase restaurant tipping*, Journal of Applied Social Psychology, 32(2), 300-309 (DOI 10.1111/j.1559-1816.2002.tb00216.x) — N: Field study in a real restaurant; ~92 dining parties across conditions (Exp 1 + Exp 2). Foundational lab precedent: Regan (1971), J Exp Soc Psychol, soft-drink-favour increased raffle-ticket compliance. https://ecommons.cornell.edu/items/49ee9efd-b182-49af-8346-a3c63391a552

**Study context:** Real restaurant diners (general adult public), naturalistic; Regan (1971) used male undergraduates in a lab compliance paradigm; Field experiment with random assignment of candy/no-candy and manner-of-giving conditions; outcome = tip percentage of bill; Low-stakes impulse context (dining gratuity). Web analogue: low-friction asks (email signup, small upgrade, referral) respond better than high-consideration purchases

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three distinct disconfirming threads. (1) An UNPUBLISHED FAILURE TO REPLICATE the candy effect by Lynn & Mongan (1996) — flagged by Lynn's OWN later paper (Mann & Lynn, 'To gift or not to gift', J. Foodservice Business Research 2019/2020) as the reason a replication was 'needed to ensure the reliability of the effect.' The senior author of the cited 2002 study himself later treated its reliability as open. (2) The original is small-N, single-site, vintage field psych: Exp 1 n=92 dining parties / 2 servers (Ithaca, 1992); Exp 2 n=80 parties / 1 server (NJ, 1998); no preregistration, p-values near .0001 but no independent direct replication of the candy paradigm has been published. (3) Mechanism contested: the conceptual replications that DID work (Frank 2019 magician souvenir; Mann & Lynn 2019/2020) attribute the gift effect to POSITIVE AFFECT/MOOD, not reciprocity, and in Mann & Lynn Study 2 the increase in tip SIZE 'was not reliable' (only tip frequency was). The closely-related reciprocity-driven compliance technique, door-in-the-face, is small in meta-analysis (Feeley et al. 2012, k=78, overall r≈.10–.13; behavioral compliance r=.052, n.s.) and concession size does NOT moderate it — undercutting the strict 'bigger/personalised gesture → proportionally bigger reciprocal action' form.

**Boundary conditions:** WHERE IT HOLDS: low-stakes, immediate, naturalistic transactions where a small unsolicited gift precedes a discretionary, low-friction return — restaurant tipping (US gratuity norm), charity direct-mail with an enclosed gift, small unexpected wage/effort bumps. Effect is strongest when (a) the gift feels personal/effortful rather than transactional/standardised — the 1+1 'extra gesture' beat the same quantity given at once; a conditional 'donate and we'll send you a gift' can BACKFIRE vs an unconditional enclosed gift; (b) the reciprocal ask is IMMEDIATE — reciprocity decays sharply with delay (no effect at 1 week in Burger et al.; PNAS shows charitable reciprocity decays over time). WHERE IT LIKELY DOES NOT TRANSFER: high-consideration / high-price purchases (the original is a sub-$1 chocolate against a dinner-bill gratuity — a pure impulse/discretionary context, not a deliberated buy); anti-social or norm-violating asks (a Coke gift did NOT buy compliance with an exam-cheating request); delayed asks; and cultures without a tipping/gratuity norm (effect measured in US dining; cross-cultural tipping norms vary widely and the original was US-only). Web analogue caution: the cited evidence base is restaurant gratuities and mail charity — a free sample/lead-magnet raising newsletter sign-ups or a small upgrade is a reasonable extrapolation, but high-ticket SaaS/considered B2B purchases sit outside the demonstrated boundary, and the meta-analytic effect for the reciprocity-as-compliance mechanism is small (r≈.10).

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Reciprocity that feels engineered/transactional can backfire: a gift made explicitly CONDITIONAL on the desired action ('donate and get X') demotivated giving vs an unconditional gift in direct-mail field tests — over-signposting the quid-pro-quo kills the felt obligation. Risk of perceived manipulation/obligation-resentment if the gift is obviously a sales lever; works best when genuinely unsolicited and modest. Effect is also perishable — capitalise immediately, as the reciprocal pull decays within days.

### Aesthetic-usability effect  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** Users perceive more visually attractive interfaces as easier to use, and this perceived usability correlates more strongly with aesthetics than with actual (inherent) usability; attractive designs also buy goodwill that masks minor usability problems.

**Apply:** Invest in visual polish (clean layout, intentional type and colour, spacing, imagery quality) because it directly raises perceived ease-of-use, trust, and tolerance for small friction — a credible first-impression lifts conversion before users have touched a single feature. Use it to win the first impression and earn patience during onboarding. Do NOT treat it as a substitute for real usability testing: beauty hides problems in research sessions, so test with tasks, not just opinion ratings.

**Evidence:** Noam Tractinsky, Adi S. Katz, Dror Ikar (2000), *What is beautiful is usable*, Interacting with Computers, 13(2), 127-145 (DOI 10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00031-X) — N: ATM-interface experiments manipulating both aesthetics and actual usability; original effect: Kurosu & Kashimura (1995), CHI, 26 ATM-layout variants rated by 252 participants. https://www.ise.bgu.ac.il/faculty/noam/papers/00_nt_ask_di_iwc.pdf

**Study context:** University students/computer users rating and using ATM interfaces; original 1995 study used 252 raters; later replications span web pages, smartphone apps (PMC 2023) and varied populations; Lab experiments crossing high/low aesthetics with high/low actual usability, measuring perceived usability before and after real use; Not a purchase paradigm; concerns perceived quality/trust of any interface — applies broadly to landing pages, signup flows, and product UI as a first-impression credibility lever

**Disconfirming evidence found:** Three serious lines of disconfirmation. (1) Reverse-causality replications: Tuch, Roth, Hornbæk, Opwis & Bargas-Avila (2012, Computers in Human Behavior 28(5), DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2012.03.024), using a crossed high/low aesthetics x high/low usability mobile-phone experiment, found aesthetics did NOT affect perceived usability directly or indirectly; instead usability drove post-use perceived beauty — i.e. "what is usable is beautiful," the OPPOSITE causal direction to Tractinsky. Hamborg, Hülsmann & Kaspar (2014, Advances in HCI, DOI 10.1155/2014/946239) replicated this reversal. (2) Explicit non-replication: the UXPA "Boundary Conditions" study (N=117, US MTurk, longitudinal healthcare-portal use) found NO significant main effect of aesthetics on SUS (F(1,113)=0.696, p=.41, eta-sq=.006) while ACTUAL manipulated usability WAS significant (F(1,113)=11.317, p=.001, eta-sq=.091) — directly contradicting Tractinsky's headline claim that aesthetics, not actual usability, drives perceived usability. (3) Confound: CHI 2023 "Statistically Controlling for Processing Fluency Reduces the Aesthetic-Usability Effect" shows the effect shrinks when processing fluency is partialled out, implying aesthetics is partly a proxy, not the driver. A 2024-era meta-analysis on aesthetics->performance (Zenodo 18777943) reports only a small-to-medium g=0.29 across 31 studies / 234 effect sizes / 18,794 participants, with HIGH unexplained heterogeneity and a flagged prior-experience confound — modest, not the strong effect folklore implies.

**Boundary conditions:** HOLDS most reliably for: PRE-USE / first-impression judgments (the correlation between perceived beauty and expected ease-of-use is large and replicates across cultures — Japan, Israel, web pages, and is reported even stronger in some non-Western samples, so it is not a Western-only artifact). Strongest where users have little or no actual interaction to draw on — landing pages, signup first impressions, app-store thumbnails, brand/credibility snap-judgments. WEAKENS OR REVERSES when: (a) users actually USE the product over time — repeated/longitudinal exposure lets real usability dominate ratings (UXPA healthcare-portal study); (b) usability problems are MAJOR not minor — beauty only buys tolerance for trivial friction, and goodwill collapses past a frustration threshold (NN/g's own Arcadis example); (c) the artifact is utilitarian/task-critical (mobile phone in Tuch 2012) where usability drives perceived beauty instead; (d) processing fluency is controlled for. This is NOT a purchase/impulse paradigm — it concerns perceived-quality/credibility, so price band and impulse-vs-considered framing don't map cleanly; treat it as a first-impression credibility lever that decays once real interaction begins. Effect on actual task PERFORMANCE (vs perception) is small (g~0.29) and heterogeneous — do not assume pretty UI makes users objectively faster.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Designers can over-index on aesthetics and let real usability defects ship because the beauty halo masks them in unmoderated testing — NN/g flags exactly this: in usability sessions participants praise attractive designs while failing tasks, hiding genuine defects from research. Beauty-bought goodwill is finite and front-loaded: it covers first impressions and minor friction, but reverses to disproportionate frustration once usability problems accumulate or recur in repeated use. Practical caution: don't substitute visual polish for usability testing, and don't trust self-reported ease-of-use on a good-looking prototype — measure task completion/time, not just perception.

### Zeigarnik effect (open-loop tension) — REPLICATION-CONTESTED  `CONTESTED`

**Claim:** The classic claim is that interrupted/incomplete tasks are remembered better and create lingering cognitive tension that pulls toward completion; a 2025 meta-analysis finds NO reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks, while the related tendency to RESUME interrupted tasks (Ovsiankina effect) does hold.

**Apply:** Lean on the part that replicates — the pull to resume — not the shaky memory claim: progress bars with visible incompletion, multi-step onboarding checklists with remaining items, 'profile 60% complete', saved-cart and resume-where-you-left-off prompts, and partially pre-filled forms all exploit the open-loop drive to finish. Avoid framing copy or strategy around 'people will remember your unfinished message better' — that specific memory effect is not supported.

**Evidence:** Lukas Ghibellini, Beat Meier (meta-analysis); original: Bluma Zeigarnik (1927) (2025), *Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects*, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications (Nature), 12, article 05000 (DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) — N: Systematic review + meta-analysis aggregating decades of Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina studies; concludes Ovsiankina (resumption) is a general tendency, Zeigarnik (recall advantage) 'lacks universal validity'. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05000-w

**Study context:** Meta-analytic aggregation across many lab samples (students/adults) since Zeigarnik's 1927 dissertation; original effect notoriously sensitive to experimenter authority, task involvement and situational demand; Predominantly lab tasks (interrupted puzzles/tasks) measuring recall and resumption; modern replications inconsistent; Not purchase-specific; web analogue is completion-drive UI (progress, checklists, saved state), where the resumption mechanism, not memory, does the work

**Disconfirming evidence found:** The claim as the checker framed it (memory effect fails, resumption holds) is itself the disconfirming evidence against the *folk* Zeigarnik claim — and it survives adversarial scrutiny. The 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) found NO reliable memory advantage for unfinished/interrupted tasks. With Zeigarnik's own 1927 dissertation removed, the aggregate interrupted-to-completed recall ratio collapses to ~0.99 (i.e. no advantage), and the meta-analysts state Zeigarnik's original computations were inflated. Independent older failures corroborate: Van Bergen (1968) ran multiple variants (subset of tasks interrupted, different materials, children) and none reproduced Zeigarnik's pattern. So the strongest disconfirming evidence is AGAINST the classic memory claim, not against the nuanced replication-contested framing here. No credible evidence was found that resurrects the memory advantage as a robust general effect; thus "memory advantage" is effectively DEBUNKED while "resumption drive" (Ovsiankina) is the surviving, reliable mechanism.

**Boundary conditions:** Where it (weakly) holds: the memory advantage surfaces mainly under a "relaxed" lab atmosphere with high experimenter authority, real task involvement, and a felt situational demand to complete — conditions common in 1920s lab settings and rare in modern neutral/anonymous testing. Under neutral framing (no manipulated atmosphere) or achievement framing (tasks pitched as an intelligence test), the memory effect disappears. Where it does NOT transfer: it is NOT a dependable general memory law, and there is no basis to assume better recall of "unfinished" content in product/marketing contexts. The RESUMPTION drive (Ovsiankina) is the part that transfers: it is the defensible engine behind completion-drive UI — progress bars, checklists, saved/restored state, "X% complete" profiles, multi-step flows with a clear next step. Evidence base is lab tasks (interrupted puzzles/manual tasks) with student/adult samples; not purchase-type-specific and not price-banded — so any application to impulse vs considered purchases, price tiers, or specific cultures is extrapolation beyond the measured base. For conversion UI: lean on the resumption/goal-gradient mechanism (people want to close an opened loop), and do NOT promise a memory/recall lift, because that specific claim does not survive the 2025 meta-analysis. Avoid manufacturing fake incompletion (artificial "you're 60% done" with no real progress) — that is a dark-pattern misuse and the genuine effect is about real open intentions.

**Side-effects / trust cost:** Side effect to flag for the UX skill: completion-drive cues exploit a real resumption tension, so overuse creates nag/anxiety and false-progress framings (fabricated "almost done" states, forced multi-step where one step would do) read as manipulative and erode trust. Keep loops genuine — the opened loop should map to real, user-valued progress. Do not market any "improves memory/recall" benefit from incompletion; that claim is not supported.
